Viscous flow – background

The mathematics behind Zaehigkeit and the viscous flux in EulerLoeser: Navier-Stokes equations in conservation form, Stokes stress and Fourier heat flux, the face gradient with non-orthogonal correction, Sutherland's law and per-cell properties, and the two touchstones Couette and Blasius – with derivations, assumptions and limits.

What this is about

The Euler equations know only convection and pressure. Friction and heat conduction enter as a second flux that depends not on the state but on its gradients. In conservation form, for each control cell VcV_c with boundary ∂Vc\partial V_c:

ddt∫VcUdV+∮∂Vc(Fc−Fv)⋅ndS=0,Fv⋅n=(0τn(τv)⋅n+k∇T⋅n).\frac{d}{dt} \int_{V_c} \mathbf{U} \, dV + \oint_{\partial V_c} \left( \mathbf{F}^{c} - \mathbf{F}^{v} \right) \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = 0, \qquad \mathbf{F}^{v} \cdot \mathbf{n} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ \mathbf{\tau} \mathbf{n} \\ (\mathbf{\tau} \mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{n} + k \, \nabla T \cdot \mathbf{n} \end{pmatrix} .

Fc\mathbf{F}^{c} is the convective flux (fluxes and reconstruction), τ\mathbf{\tau} the stress tensor, kk the thermal conductivity. Mass has no viscous part. The flux is switched on by a viscosity in the settings; the case file sets it through the Reynolds number.

Stress and heat flux

For a Newtonian fluid the stress is linear in the rate of strain Sij=12(∂ui/∂xj+∂uj/∂xi)S_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}(\partial u_i/\partial x_j + \partial u_j/\partial x_i). Isotropy allows only two coefficients, τij=2μSij+λ(∇⋅v)δij\tau_{ij} = 2\mu S_{ij} + \lambda \, (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}) \, \delta_{ij}. Stokes' hypothesis demands that the mean normal stress be zero, so that pressure alone carries the mechanical mean stress: τii=(2μ+3λ)∇⋅v=0\tau_{ii} = (2\mu + 3\lambda) \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0, hence λ=−23μ\lambda = -\frac{2}{3}\mu and

τij=μ(∂ui∂xj+∂uj∂xi)−23μ(∇⋅v)δij.\tau_{ij} = \mu \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i} \right) - \frac{2}{3} \mu \, (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}) \, \delta_{ij} .

The heat flux follows Fourier, q=−k∇T\mathbf{q} = -k \nabla T; it enters the flux with a positive sign because −q-\mathbf{q} points into the cell when TT grows outward. The work of the stresses at the face is (τv)⋅n(\mathbf{\tau} \mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{n}, with the face value of v\mathbf{v}.

Two simple cases show the coefficients: shear u=ayu = a y gives τxy=μa\tau_{xy} = \mu a and, at a face with n=ey\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{e}_y, the work uμau \mu a; pure strain w=azw = a z gives τzz=2μa−23μa=43μa\tau_{zz} = 2\mu a - \frac{2}{3}\mu a = \frac{4}{3}\mu a.

Assumptions. Newtonian fluid, local equilibrium, Stokes' hypothesis (zero bulk viscosity – exact for monatomic gases, good for diatomic ones at moderate dilatation rates). No radiative or diffusive heat flux here; the diffusive part of enthalpy is added for mixtures (species).

Viscosity and conduction

There are three kinds of viscosity: constant, after Sutherland, and from the properties per cell. Sutherland follows from the kinetic theory of gases with a weakly attractive potential: the hard sphere gives μ∝T\mu \propto \sqrt{T}, the attraction enlarges the collision cross-section by the factor 1+S/T1 + S/T, hence

μ(T)=μref⁡(TTref⁡)3/2Tref⁡+ST+S,S=110.4K(air).\mu(T) = \mu_{\mathrm{ref}} \left( \frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}} \right)^{3/2} \frac{T_{\mathrm{ref}} + S}{T + S}, \qquad S = 110.4 \ \mathrm{K} \ \text{(air)} .

The conductivity follows from a fixed Prandtl number, k=μcp/Pr⁡k = \mu c_p / \mathrm{Pr} with cp=γR/(γ−1)c_p = \gamma R/(\gamma - 1). With eddy viscosity (turbulence) the stress carries μ+μt\mu + \mu_t and the heat flux cp(μ/Pr⁡+μt/Prt⁡)c_p(\mu/\mathrm{Pr} + \mu_t/\mathrm{Pr}_t) (Boussinesq, Prt⁡=0.9\mathrm{Pr}_t = 0.9; the part 23ρk\frac{2}{3}\rho k of the turbulent normal stress is left out).

With properties per cell (mixtures, real gases) the solver gets μ\mu and kk per cell from the mixture transport (species) or from the real-gas model; at the face they are averaged. The Prandtl number is then no input but follows from μcp/k\mu c_p/k.

Assumptions. Sutherland holds for air from about 100 K to 2000 K within a few percent; the constant is fixed. A fixed Prandtl number is good for air (0.71 to 0.72 over a wide range), not for mixtures with hydrogen – use the properties per cell there.

Gradient at the face

The flux needs ∇u,∇v,∇w,∇T\nabla u, \nabla v, \nabla w, \nabla T at the face. The plain average of the two cell gradients is consistent but couples neighbouring cells poorly: a checkerboard field has zero gradient in every cell. Therefore the component along the line between cell centres, unit vector e\mathbf{e} and length ℓ\ell, is replaced by the difference of the values:

∇ϕf=∇ϕ+(ϕN−ϕOℓ−∇ϕ⋅e)e,∇ϕ=12(∇ϕO+∇ϕN).\nabla \phi_f = \overline{\nabla \phi} + \left( \frac{\phi_N - \phi_O}{\ell} - \overline{\nabla \phi} \cdot \mathbf{e} \right) \mathbf{e}, \qquad \overline{\nabla \phi} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \nabla \phi_O + \nabla \phi_N \right) .

Derivation: for a linear field ϕN−ϕO=ℓ∇ϕ⋅e\phi_N - \phi_O = \ell \, \nabla\phi \cdot \mathbf{e} holds exactly, the bracket vanishes and the face gradient is exact. For a checkerboard field ∇ϕ=0\overline{\nabla \phi} = 0, but (ϕN−ϕO)/ℓ≠0(\phi_N - \phi_O)/\ell \ne 0 – the coupling remains. On orthogonal meshes (e=n\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{n}) the normal component is the compact two-point difference. At the boundary the boundary value replaces the neighbour cell, with the distance to the face centre; at periodic faces the partner's state and gradient are rotated into the own frame.

What follows. The viscous flux is second order as long as the cell gradients are (reconstruction), and the coupling of neighbours stays stable on tetrahedral and prism meshes too. For strongly skewed cells (large angle between e\mathbf{e} and n\mathbf{n}) the transverse part carries much and the accuracy depends on the cell gradients.

Touchstones: Couette and Blasius

Couette with viscous heating. Between two plates a distance hh apart, the lower at rest, the upper moving with UU, both at TwT_w. Without a pressure gradient τxy\tau_{xy} is constant, so u=Uy/hu = U y/h. Energy says kT''+μ(U/h)2=0k T'' + \mu (U/h)^2 = 0 (dissipation equals conduction), with T(0)=T(h)=TwT(0) = T(h) = T_w:

T(y)=Tw+μU22kyh(1−yh),Tmax⁡−Tw=μU28k=Pr⁡U28cp.T(y) = T_w + \frac{\mu U^2}{2k} \frac{y}{h} \left( 1 - \frac{y}{h} \right), \qquad T_{\max} - T_w = \frac{\mu U^2}{8k} = \frac{\mathrm{Pr} \, U^2}{8 c_p} .

The wall force is μU/h\mu U/h per area. Both are met by the probe to within a few per mille.

Blasius. The flat plate without pressure gradient has the similarity solution u/U=f'(η)u/U = f'(\eta), η=yU/(νx)\eta = y\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}, with

f'''+12ff''=0,f(0)=f'(0)=0,f'(∞)=1⇒f''(0)=0.332057,cf=2f''(0)Rex⁡=0.664Rex⁡.f''' + \frac{1}{2} f f'' = 0, \qquad f(0) = f'(0) = 0, \quad f'(\infty) = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad f''(0) = 0.332057, \qquad c_f = \frac{2 f''(0)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}} = \frac{0.664}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}} .

The probe solves the equation itself (shooting) and compares profile and wall friction of the computation with it.

What follows. Couette checks stress, work and conduction together and exactly (the profile is quadratic, the second-order scheme meets it up to the boundary error). Blasius checks the coupling with convection: a laminar boundary layer needs about ten to twenty cells across the wall, otherwise cfc_f is too small.

The recalculation of the derivations (Stokes trace, Couette profile, Blasius value, exactness of the face gradient for linear fields) is in docs/theorie/rechnung/cfd_viskos.py.

Limits

  • No bulk viscosity, no non-Newtonian fluids.
  • Sutherland with a fixed constant; outside roughly 100 K to 2000 K use the properties per cell.
  • The turbulent normal stress 23ρk\frac{2}{3}\rho k is not in the stress tensor; with two-equation models think of it as contained in the pressure.
