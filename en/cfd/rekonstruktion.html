Gradients and limiters – background

The computation behind Gradienten and Limiter: how cell means become linear profiles and thus second order, Green-Gauss and weighted least squares with their exactness properties, the limiters of Barth and Jespersen and of Venkatakrishnan with the reference scale, and the vector limiting of velocity – with derivations, assumptions, checks and limits.

What this is about

A finite-volume method knows only the mean per cell. If the numerical flux takes these values unchanged as the states left and right of the face, the method is first order: the error falls with the cell size hh, not with h2h^2, and shocks and boundary layers smear over many cells. Second order arises when a linear profile is assumed in every cell (MUSCL, van Leer):

wf=wc+φc∇wc⋅(xf−xc).w_f = w_c + \varphi_c\,\nabla w_c\cdot(\mathbf x_f - \mathbf x_c).

The primitive quantities (ρ,u,v,w,p)(\rho, u, v, w, p) are reconstructed, at each face from both sides. Two building blocks are needed: a gradient ∇wc\nabla w_c from the neighbour values and a limiter φc∈[0,1]\varphi_c \in [0, 1] that backs off at discontinuities just enough that no new extrema arise. If a reconstructed density or pressure drops to zero or below, the solver uses the cell values at that face.

Gradients

Green-Gauss

By Gauss's theorem ∫V∇wdV=∮∂VwdS\int_V \nabla w\,dV = \oint_{\partial V} w\,d\mathbf S. With the mean of the gradient and one value per face,

∇wc≈1Vc∑fwfSf,wf=12(wc+wn) inside,wf=wboundary at the boundary.\nabla w_c \approx \frac{1}{V_c}\sum_{f} w_f\,\mathbf S_f, \qquad w_f = \tfrac12(w_c + w_n) \text{ inside}, \qquad w_f = w_{\text{boundary}} \text{ at the boundary}.

Sf\mathbf S_f points out of the cell. The mean of the two cell values is the value at the face centre only if the face centre lies midway between the cell centres – on uniform grids. There the formula becomes the central difference and is even exact for quadratic fields. On distorted tetrahedra it deviates already for linear fields: by ∇w⋅(xf−x)\nabla w\cdot(\mathbf x_f - \bar{\mathbf x}) per face, with x\bar{\mathbf x} the mean of the two cell centres. At periodic boundaries, where the boundary value is the state of the partner cell, the mean is taken there too.

Weighted least squares

The gradient should predict the neighbour values from the own value as well as possible. With the distance vectors dk\mathbf d_k from the cell centre to the neighbour centres (at the boundary to the face centre, at periodic boundaries to the mapped centre of the partner cell) and the weights gk=1/|dk|2g_k = 1/|\mathbf d_k|^2,

ming⁡∑kgk(wc+g⋅dk−wk)2⟹Ag=b,A=∑kgkdkdkT,b=∑kgkdk(wk−wc).\min_{\mathbf g}\ \sum_k g_k\big(w_c + \mathbf g\cdot\mathbf d_k - w_k\big)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\,\mathbf g = \mathbf b, \qquad A = \sum_k g_k\,\mathbf d_k\mathbf d_k^{\mathsf T}, \quad \mathbf b = \sum_k g_k\,\mathbf d_k\,(w_k - w_c).

AA depends only on the grid; its inverse is computed once per cell and stored (9 values), every gradient is then a product with b\mathbf b. Two properties follow directly:

  • Exact for linear fields on any grid: if w=a+c⋅xw = a + \mathbf c\cdot\mathbf x, then wk−wc=c⋅dkw_k - w_c = \mathbf c\cdot\mathbf d_k, the sum of squares for g=c\mathbf g = \mathbf c is zero and the minimum unique as long as AA is regular.
  • Central difference on uniform grids: with six neighbours at distance hh, A=2IA = 2I, b\mathbf b the sum of the differences – the gradient becomes (w+x−w−x)/(2h)(w_{+x} - w_{-x})/(2h) and is exact for quadratic fields.

The weight 1/|d|21/|\mathbf d|^2 makes the equations scale-independent and gives near neighbours more weight – important at stretched cells in boundary layers. AA is always regular for a closed cell because the distance vectors to its faces span space; the check for |det⁡A|≤10−300|\det A| \le 10^{-300} with the message "gradient matrix singular" is a safeguard that valid grids do not reach. Least squares is the solver's default.

Limiters

For each cell the minimum and maximum of the own and all neighbour values are found (at the boundary the boundary values), Δmax⁡=wmax⁡−wc\Delta_{\max} = w_{\max} - w_c and Δmin⁡=wmin⁡−wc\Delta_{\min} = w_{\min} - w_c. At each face Δ2=∇wc⋅(xf−xc)\Delta_2 = \nabla w_c\cdot(\mathbf x_f - \mathbf x_c) is the unlimited increment and

Δ1={Δmax⁡,Δ2>0,Δmin⁡,Δ2<0,\Delta_1 = \begin{cases}\Delta_{\max}, & \Delta_2 > 0,\\ \Delta_{\min}, & \Delta_2 < 0,\end{cases}

the increment that just does not create a new extremum. The factor of the cell is the minimum of the face factors, starting at 1 (faces with Δ2=0\Delta_2 = 0 are ignored).

Barth and Jespersen

φf=min⁡(1,Δ1Δ2).\varphi_f = \min\Big(1, \frac{\Delta_1}{\Delta_2}\Big).

The reconstructed value stays exactly within the range of the neighbourhood. The limiter is not differentiable and acts at smooth extrema as well; steady computations converge poorly with it because the factor can jump between iterations.

Venkatakrishnan

Venkatakrishnan (1995) replaces the minimum by a smooth function:

φf=Δ12+ε2+2Δ1Δ2Δ12+2Δ22+Δ1Δ2+ε2,ε2=(Kh)3,h3=Vc.\varphi_f = \frac{\Delta_1^2 + \varepsilon^2 + 2\,\Delta_1\Delta_2}{\Delta_1^2 + 2\Delta_2^2 + \Delta_1\Delta_2 + \varepsilon^2}, \qquad \varepsilon^2 = (K h)^3, \quad h^3 = V_c.

Without ε\varepsilon and with r=Δ1/Δ2r = \Delta_1/\Delta_2, φ=(r2+2r)/(r2+r+2)\varphi = (r^2 + 2r)/(r^2 + r + 2): φ(1)=3/4\varphi(1) = 3/4, φ(2)=1\varphi(2) = 1, slightly above 1 for r>2r > 2 and tending to 1 – starting the minimum over the faces at 1 caps that. φΔ2≤Δ1\varphi\,\Delta_2 \le \Delta_1 then holds for all rr: no new extrema. The term ε2\varepsilon^2 switches the limiter off in smooth regions where the differences are small against ε\varepsilon; in exchange it admits small overshoots there. The constant KK (default 1, usually 0.3 to 5) chooses the compromise between convergence and monotonicity.

Reference scale. (Kh)3(Kh)^3 has the dimension of a length cubed, Δ2\Delta^2 that of the variable squared. This fits only if the variables are dimensionless and of order 1. Computed in pascal, (Kh)3(Kh)^3 is zero against 1010Pa2⁡10^{10}\,\mathrm{Pa}^2, and the limiter acts everywhere like Barth-Jespersen. With a reference scale ss per variable, ε2=(Kh)3s2\varepsilon^2 = (Kh)^3 s^2 (bezug).

Velocity as a vector

Limited per Cartesian component, the result depends on the orientation of the coordinate system: a sector of a turbomachine rotated by 30° gives a different solution than the full annulus. With vektor(true) the velocity gets one common factor: the bound is maxk⁡|uk−uc|\max_k|\mathbf u_k - \mathbf u_c|, compared at every face with |(∇u)(xf−xc)||(\nabla\mathbf u)\,(\mathbf x_f - \mathbf x_c)|. Both quantities are rotation-invariant.

Assumptions

  • The cell centre is the centroid (grid); only then is the cell mean of a linear field its value at the cell centre and the reconstruction second order.
  • The boundary values are values at the face centre (or, at periodic boundaries, at the mapped partner centre).
  • The limiter looks only at the immediate neighbours across faces, not across nodes.

What follows

  • Least squares on unstructured grids. Green-Gauss with the mean of the cell values loses accuracy on distorted tetrahedra even for linear fields; only on uniform grids are both equally good.
  • Venkatakrishnan for steady computations, with KK between 1 and 5; Barth-Jespersen when strict monotonicity is required and convergence is secondary.
  • Computing with dimensions means setting reference scales. Without them Venkatakrishnan turns into Barth-Jespersen in pascal.
  • Rotated sectors should use vector limiting, so that sector and annulus give the same result.
  • A limiter keeps second order in smooth regions only with Venkatakrishnan and sufficient ε\varepsilon; the Eulerprobe measures the order with K=5K = 5 and AUSM+-up.

How it is checked

  • TheorieC1probe (rekonstruktion): quadratic field on uniform hexahedra (both methods exact in 64 inner cells), linear field on distorted tetrahedra (least squares exact, Green-Gauss not), the four limiter cases of the table by hand, names and settings.
  • Eulerprobe: linear field exact on distorted tetrahedra, limiter without new extrema at the jump, second order with manufactured solutions, reference scale.
  • Periodenprobe: gradient at the periodic boundary, vector limiting in the rotated sector.
  • Recalculation docs/theorie/rechnung/cfd_rekonstruktion.py: central difference and Green-Gauss symbolically for a general quadratic field, both forms of the limiter, values of the table, φ(r)\varphi(r).

Limits

  • Green-Gauss uses the mean of the cell values, without a correction for the position of the face centre; on skewed grids the gradient is then not exact even for linear fields.
  • The limiter takes the neighbours across faces; node neighbours (a larger stencil) are not available.
  • Barth-Jespersen can stall steady convergence after a few orders of magnitude (limiter cycling); in the Newton method the factor can be frozen for that reason.
