Gaussian process regression – background

The computation behind Gaussprozess: kernels, prediction via the Cholesky decomposition, marginal likelihood and the choice of hyperparameters – with pseudocode, checks and limits.

What it is about

A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions: any finite set of function values is jointly normal, with a covariance that the kernel k(x,x')k(x, x') determines from the distance of the points. From noisy measurements yi=f(xi)+εiy_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i a normal distribution for ff follows at every new point – a mean as surrogate model and a variance that grows between the data points. This is typical for expensive computations or experiments where one wants to know how reliable the interpolation is.

Kernels

With r=‖x−x'‖r = \|x - x'\|, length ℓ\ell and signal variance σf2\sigma_f^2:

kSE=σf2e−r2/(2ℓ2),k3/2=σf2(1+3rℓ)e−3r/ℓ,k5/2=σf2(1+5rℓ+5r23ℓ2)e−5r/ℓ.k_{SE} = \sigma_f^2\, e^{-r^2/(2\ell^2)}, \qquad k_{3/2} = \sigma_f^2\Big(1 + \frac{\sqrt3 r}{\ell}\Big)e^{-\sqrt3 r/\ell}, \qquad k_{5/2} = \sigma_f^2\Big(1 + \frac{\sqrt5 r}{\ell} + \frac{5r^2}{3\ell^2}\Big)e^{-\sqrt5 r/\ell}.

The squared-exponential kernel gives infinitely smooth functions, the Matérn kernels once or twice differentiable ones – often more realistic for measured data. Noise enters as σn2I\sigma_n^2 I on the covariance of the measurements.

Derivation. The Matérn family is kν(r)=σf221−νΓ(ν)uνKν(u)k_\nu(r) = \sigma_f^2\,\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\,u^\nu K_\nu(u) with u=2νr/ℓu = \sqrt{2\nu}\,r/\ell and the modified Bessel function KνK_\nu. For half-integer ν=p+12\nu = p + \tfrac12 the series of KνK_\nu terminates and gives a polynomial of degree pp times e−ue^{-u} – the two formulas above for p=1,2p = 1, 2; for ν→∞\nu \to \infty the squared-exponential kernel results. Smoothness is read off at the origin. Expanding around r=0r = 0,

k3/2=σf2(1−3r22ℓ2+O(r3)),k5/2=σf2(1−5r26ℓ2+O(r4));k_{3/2} = \sigma_f^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{3r^2}{2\ell^2} + O(r^3)\Bigr), \qquad k_{5/2} = \sigma_f^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{5r^2}{6\ell^2} + O(r^4)\Bigr);

both have k'(0)=0k'(0) = 0, so the process is mean-square differentiable. The r3r^3 term at ν=3/2\nu = 3/2 prevents the second derivative; at ν=5/2\nu = 5/2 it is absent. In general a Matérn process is mm times differentiable if ν>m\nu > m.

Prediction and marginal likelihood

With Kij=k(xi,xj)K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j), the vector k*=k(xi,x*)k_* = k(x_i, x_*) and the Cholesky decomposition LLT=K+σn2IL L^T = K + \sigma_n^2 I (Rasmussen and Williams, algorithm 2.1):

α=L−TL−1y,f*=k*Tα,Var⁡f*=k(x*,x*)−vTv,v=L−1k*,\alpha = L^{-T}L^{-1}y, \qquad \bar f_* = k_*^T\alpha, \qquad \operatorname{Var} f_* = k(x_*, x_*) - v^T v,\ v = L^{-1}k_*,

Derivation. Measurements and the new function value are jointly normal,

(yf*)∼N(0,(K+σn2Ik*k*Tk(x*,x*))),\begin{pmatrix} y \\ f_* \end{pmatrix} \sim N\!\left(0,\ \begin{pmatrix} K + \sigma_n^2 I & k_* \\ k_*^T & k(x_*, x_*) \end{pmatrix}\right),

and the conditional normal distribution of f*f_* given yy has mean k*T(K+σn2I)−1yk_*^T(K + \sigma_n^2I)^{-1}y and variance k(x*,x*)−k*T(K+σn2I)−1k*k(x_*, x_*) - k_*^T(K + \sigma_n^2I)^{-1}k_* (Schur complement). With LLT=K+σn2ILL^T = K + \sigma_n^2 I, k*T(LLT)−1k*=vTvk_*^T(LL^T)^{-1}k_* = v^Tv – a sum of squares, so the variance cannot become negative through cancellation.

log⁡p(y∣X)=−12yTα−∑ilog⁡Lii−n2log⁡2π.\log p(y \mid X) = -\tfrac12\,y^T\alpha - \sum_i \log L_{ii} - \tfrac n2 \log 2\pi.

This is the logarithm of the normal density N(0,K+σn2I)N(0, K + \sigma_n^2I) at yy, with log⁡det⁡(LLT)=2∑ilog⁡Lii\log\det(LL^T) = 2\sum_i\log L_{ii}. The first term rewards the fit to the data, the second penalises a model that considers many functions possible (large determinant) – the marginal likelihood thus chooses by itself between too smooth and too wiggly.

With a constant mean the mean of the data is subtracted first and added back afterwards; far from all data the prediction tends to it. The hyperparameters (σf,ℓ,σn)(\sigma_f, \ell, \sigma_n) are chosen by optimiere: maximum of the marginal likelihood in logarithms (hence positive) with L-BFGS from framework-math, numerical gradient.

K ← k(x_i, x_j) + σ_n² I;  L ← Cholesky(K)            if it fails: message
α ← L⁻ᵀ (L⁻¹ (y − ȳ))
predict(x*): k* ← k(x_i, x*);  v ← L⁻¹ k*;  mean ȳ + k*ᵀ α;  variance k(x*, x*) − vᵀv
optimise: θ ← log(σ_f, ℓ, σ_n);  max_θ log p(y | X, θ) with L-BFGS

Accuracy and cost. The Cholesky decomposition costs n3/3n^3/3 operations, each prediction afterwards O(n)O(n) for the mean and O(n2)O(n^2) for the variance. The kernel matrix of a smooth kernel is very badly conditioned for densely spaced points (for the squared-exponential kernel the condition number grows exponentially with the density); therefore a jitter 10−12σf210^{-12}\sigma_f^2 is added to the diagonal even without noise. It acts like a tiny measurement noise: at the data points a relative error of this size remains, and the variance there is not exactly zero but at most of this order.

What follows. Without noise the Gaussian process interpolates (variance zero at the data points), with noise it smooths; at a single data point the variance is σf2σn2/(σf2+σn2)\sigma_f^2\sigma_n^2/(\sigma_f^2 + \sigma_n^2). Far from all data k*→0k_* \to 0: the mean tends to the data mean, the variance to σf2\sigma_f^2 – the Gaussian process says that it knows nothing there. It is therefore only good for extrapolation over about one length ℓ\ell.

How it is checked

  • One data point, all three kernels: mean k*y/(k+σn2)k_* y/(k + \sigma_n^2) and variance σf2−k*2/(k+σn2)\sigma_f^2 - k_*^2/(k + \sigma_n^2) by hand to 1e-14.
  • Marginal likelihood of two points with closed-form 2×2 inverse to 1e-13.
  • Noise-free (Matérn 5/2): the 12 data values met to 1e-8, variance there below 1e-8, positive in between; far away the mean of the data.
  • Hyperparameters from 40 noisy points: every neighbouring setting (±10 %) has smaller marginal likelihood; noise variance met within a factor of 2.

gp.TheorieCprobe (2 checks): all three kernels at r=ℓr = \ell and Matérn 5/2 at r=2ℓr = 2\ell against the closed-form values (10−1410^{-14}), k(x,x)=σf2k(x, x) = \sigma_f^2 without noise; the curvature at the origin −3/ℓ2-3/\ell^2 and −5/(3ℓ2)-5/(3\ell^2) from the difference quotient. The counter-check replaces 5r2/35r^2/3 by 5r2/25r^2/2 and is detected.

The recomputation with sympy and numpy (prediction from the conditional normal distribution, log⁡det⁡=2∑log⁡Lii\log\det = 2\sum\log L_{ii}, vTv=k*TK−1k*v^Tv = k_*^TK^{-1}k_*, limits of the Matérn derivatives) is in the source tree under docs/theorie/rechnung/stats_gauss.py.

Limits

Dense Cholesky decomposition: effort n3n^3, sensible up to a few thousand points. Isotropic kernels (one length for all directions); no derivative observations.
