Distributions and quantiles – background

How the 14 distributions of the module compute density, distribution function, quantile and random number: the reduction to the special functions, the normal quantile after Acklam with a Halley step, bracketing with Brent, the discrete quantile by binary search and the random numbers from Rng.

What this is about

Every distribution in the module implements the interface Distribution with seven methods: density pdf (for discrete distributions the probability of a value), distribution function cdf, quantile quantile, mean, variance, sd and sample. The names follow R: pdf corresponds to d…, cdf to p…, quantile to q…, sample to r….

The distribution function F(x)=P(X≤x)F(x) = P(X \le x) answers “how likely is a value up to xx?” – p-values come from it. The quantile is the inverse: F−1(p)F^{-1}(p) is the value below which the fraction pp of the distribution lies – critical values and interval limits come from it. For discrete distributions, whose FF is a staircase, the quantile is the smallest value kk with F(k)≥pF(k) \ge p.

The work is divided up:

  • The distribution function comes, where there is no closed form, from the regularised incomplete gamma and beta functions (see Special functions).
  • The quantile comes from a closed-form inverse, from the rational approximation after Acklam (normal, lognormal), from a root search on F(x)−pF(x) - p (continuous distributions without a closed inverse) or from a binary search (discrete distributions).
  • The random number comes by default from inversion F−1(U)F^{-1}(U); some distributions have faster ways of their own.

Where the reductions come from, taking the t distribution as an example: the density is proportional to (1+t2/ν)−(ν+1)/2(1+t^2/\nu)^{-(\nu+1)/2}. With the substitution y=ν/(ν+t2)y = \nu/(\nu+t^2) the integral over the tail t>|t0|t > |t_0| becomes a beta integral, and P(|T|>|t0|)=Iy(ν2,12)P(|T| > |t_0|) = I_y(\frac\nu2, \frac12). Half of it is the upper tail. Similarly the binomial sum ∑i≤kC(n,i)pi(1−p)n−i\sum_{i \le k} C(n,i)\,p^i(1-p)^{n-i} turns into I1−p(n−k,k+1)I_{1-p}(n-k, k+1) by repeated integration by parts; the sum is therefore never formed term by term.

Survival function

For p-values one mostly needs the upper tail F(x)=P(X>x)=1−F(x)\bar F(x) = P(X > x) = 1 - F(x). Computed as a difference it vanishes as soon as F(x)F(x) equals 1 in double: 1−Φ(9)1 - \Phi(9) gives 0 instead of 1.13⋅10−191.13\cdot10^{-19}. Hence sf(x). Normal, t, F and chi-squared compute the upper tail directly: Φ(z)=12erfc⁡(z/2)\bar\Phi(z) = \frac12\operatorname{erfc}(z/\sqrt2); t for t>0t > 0 as 12Iy(ν2,12)\frac12 I_y(\frac\nu2, \frac12) (see above); F as Iν2/(ν2+ν1x)(ν22,ν12)I_{\nu_2/(\nu_2+\nu_1x)}(\frac{\nu_2}2, \frac{\nu_1}2); chi-squared as Q(ν2,x2)Q(\frac\nu2, \frac x2). The other distributions inherit the default 1−F(x)1 - F(x) from the interface. Since finding WT10 all p-values that come from the normal, t, F or chi-squared distribution (tests, regression, correlation, analysis of variance) go through sf.

Densities

All densities containing a gamma or beta function are formed logarithmically and exponentiated only at the end, for instance for the chi-squared distribution

f(x)=exp⁡((k−1)ln⁡x−x2−kln⁡2−ln⁡Γ(k)),k=ν/2f(x) = \exp\left((k-1)\ln x - \frac x2 - k\ln 2 - \ln\Gamma(k)\right), \qquad k = \nu/2

and for the binomial distribution the binomial coefficient C(n,k)=n!k!(n−k)!C(n,k) = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} via ln⁡Γ(n+1)−ln⁡Γ(k+1)−ln⁡Γ(n−k+1)\ln\Gamma(n+1) - \ln\Gamma(k+1) - \ln\Gamma(n-k+1). Values like C(1000,500)C(1000, 500), whose magnitude would lie far outside double, remain computable.

Framework convention: The parameters are named as in R, but through class constructors: Gamma(shape, scale) with scale θ (not rate), mean kθk\theta; Exponential(rate) with rate λ; Weibull(shape, scale); NegativeBinomial(r, p) counts the failures before the rr-th success (mean r(1−p)/pr(1-p)/p); Hypergeometric(populationN, successesK, drawsN). Parameters are not validated; invalid values lead to NaN.

The normal quantile after Acklam with a Halley step

The normal quantile zp=Φ−1(p)z_p = \Phi^{-1}(p) is the one needed most often in the module: for confidence intervals, Q-Q plots, the Wilson and Agresti-Coull intervals, the BCa correction and the coefficients of the Shapiro-Wilk test. It is computed in two stages.

Stage 1: rational approximation

P. J. Acklam gave an approximation by quotients of polynomials, split into three regions. In the middle, for 0.02425≤p≤0.975750.02425 \le p \le 0.97575, with q=p−12q = p - \frac12 and r=q2r = q^2:

z≈qa0r5+a1r4+a2r3+a3r2+a4r+a5b0r5+b1r4+b2r3+b3r2+b4r+1z \approx q\,\frac{a_0 r^5 + a_1 r^4 + a_2 r^3 + a_3 r^2 + a_4 r + a_5}{b_0 r^5 + b_1 r^4 + b_2 r^3 + b_3 r^2 + b_4 r + 1}

In the tails, for p<0.02425p < 0.02425, with q=−2ln⁡pq = \sqrt{-2\ln p}:

z≈c0q5+c1q4+c2q3+c3q2+c4q+c5d0q4+d1q3+d2q2+d3q+1z \approx \frac{c_0 q^5 + c_1 q^4 + c_2 q^3 + c_3 q^2 + c_4 q + c_5}{d_0 q^4 + d_1 q^3 + d_2 q^2 + d_3 q + 1}

and for p>0.97575p > 0.97575 the same with q=−2ln⁡(1−p)q = \sqrt{-2\ln(1-p)} and reversed sign. The middle branch is an odd function of p−12p - \frac12, hence exactly symmetric. The approximation alone has a relative error of about 10−910^{-9}.

Stage 2: one Halley step

Newton's method would improve zz with e=Φ(z)−pe = \Phi(z) - p by e/φ(z)e/\varphi(z). Halley's method also takes the second derivative into account; for the normal distribution φ'(z)=−zφ(z)\varphi'(z) = -z\varphi(z), and the step becomes

u=e2πez2/2=eφ(z),z←z−u1+zu/2u = e\,\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{z^2/2} = \frac{e}{\varphi(z)}, \qquad z \leftarrow z - \frac{u}{1 + z u / 2}

Halley converges cubically: after one step an error of 10−910^{-9} turns into errors at the level of machine precision. The prerequisite is that Φ(z)−p\Phi(z) - p is computed accurately – in the tail therefore with erfc, not with 1−erf⁡1 - \operatorname{erf}. As long as that was not the case (finding ST1), the Halley step pulled the quantile away from the correct value at p=10−10p = 10^{-10}.

Pseudocode – Dist.normalQuantile(p)

if p ≤ 0: return −∞;   if p ≥ 1: return +∞          NaN fails both comparisons and stays NaN
if p < 0.02425:
    q ← √(−2 ln p)
    z ← C(q) / D(q)                                   polynomials with c[0..5], d[0..3]
else if p ≤ 0.97575:
    q ← p − 0.5;  r ← q²
    z ← A(r)·q / B(r)                                 polynomials with a[0..5], b[0..4]
else
    q ← √(−2 ln(1−p))
    z ← −C(q) / D(q)
// one Halley step
e ← ½·erfc(−z/√2) − p                                  = Φ(z) − p, accurate in the tail too
u ← e · √(2π) · exp(z²/2)
z ← z − u / (1 + z·u/2)
return z

Quantiles of continuous distributions: bracketing and Brent

For t, chi-squared, F, gamma and beta there is neither a closed-form inverse nor a built-in approximation. The quantile is then the root of

f(x)=F(x)−pf(x) = F(x) - p

Because FF is monotonically increasing there is exactly one. Dist.bracketQuantile finds it in two steps:

  1. Establish a bracket. Each distribution passes an initial bracket [lo,hi][lo, hi] (see the table above). If the root is not inside, the bracket is widened: as long as f(lo)>0f(lo) > 0, lolo moves down by max⁡(1,hi−lo)\max(1, hi-lo); as long as f(hi)<0f(hi) < 0, hihi moves up correspondingly. Since the step is the current width, the bracket roughly doubles with each step. Each direction has at most 200 widenings.
  2. Root by Brent. Brent's method (from framework-math, Optimize.brentRoot) combines bisection, secant and inverse quadratic interpolation. It always keeps a bracket with a sign change, so it never escapes, and it converges superlinearly in smooth regions. The tolerance is 10−1210^{-12} in xx, at most 200 steps.

Pseudocode – Dist.bracketQuantile(dist, p, lo, hi)

if p ≤ 0: return lo;   if p ≥ 1: return hi
f(x) ← dist.cdf(x) − p
repeat at most 200 times while f(lo) > 0:  lo ← lo − max(1, hi − lo)
repeat at most 200 times while f(hi) < 0:  hi ← hi + max(1, hi − lo)
return Optimize.brentRoot(f, lo, hi, 1e−12)         NaN if there is no sign change

Source: envisionz-framework-stats/src/envisionz/stats/dist/Dist.java, method bracketQuantile; envisionz-framework-math/src/envisionz/math/optimierung/Optimize.java, method brentRoot.

Framework convention: For p≤0p \le 0 and p≥1p \ge 1, bracketQuantile returns the bracket limit, not ±∞\pm\infty. StudentT.quantile catches these cases beforehand and returns ±∞\pm\infty; chi-squared, F, gamma and beta return 0 at p=0p = 0 and the upper initial limit at p=1p = 1 (for beta therefore 1, but for F 10).

Quantiles of discrete distributions: binary search

For binomial, Poisson, hypergeometric and negative binomial the quantile is the smallest integer kk with F(k)≥pF(k) \ge p. Since FF is monotone, a binary search over the integers of the search range suffices: ⌈log2⁡(hi−lo+1)⌉\lceil\log_2(hi - lo + 1)\rceil evaluations of FF.

function discreteQuantile(dist, p, lo, hi):
    if p ≤ 0: return lo;   if p ≥ 1: return hi
    a ← lo;  b ← hi
    while a < b:
        m ← ⌊(a + b)/2⌋
        if dist.cdf(m) ≥ p: b ← m  else a ← m + 1
    return a

Source: envisionz-framework-stats/src/envisionz/stats/dist/Dist.java, method discreteQuantile. Each distribution sets its own upper search limit (table above); for Poisson and negative binomial it is the mean plus ten standard deviations plus 20.

This definition matches R: if pp lies exactly on a step, p=F(3)p = F(3), the result is 3; a pp just above gives 4.

Random numbers

Rng wraps a java.util.SplittableRandom with an explicitly supplied seed. Same seed means a bit-identical sequence – the basis for bootstrap, permutation test and k-means being repeatable. An Rng is not thread-safe; each run needs its own instance.

Gamma after Marsaglia and Tsang

For k≥1k \ge 1 one sets d=k−13d = k - \frac13, c=1/9dc = 1/\sqrt{9d}, draws a standard normal number xx and forms v=(1+cx)3v = (1 + cx)^3. The candidate dvd\,v is almost gamma distributed; an acceptance-rejection check makes it exact. Most candidates are already accepted by the quick pre-test u<1−0.0331x4u < 1 - 0.0331\,x^4, without a logarithm. For k<1k < 1 one draws from gamma(k+1)(k+1) and multiplies by U1/kU^{1/k}.

function Gamma.sample(rng):
    if k < 1: return Gamma(k+1, θ).sample(rng) · U^(1/k)
    d ← k − 1/3;  c ← 1/√(9d)
    repeat:
        repeat: x ← normal number;  v ← 1 + c·x   until v > 0
        v ← v³;  u ← uniform number
        if u < 1 − 0.0331·x⁴: return d·v·θ                           quick pre-test
        if ln u < x²/2 + d·(1 − v + ln v): return d·v·θ

Source: envisionz-framework-stats/src/envisionz/stats/dist/Gamma.java, method sample; envisionz-framework-stats/src/envisionz/stats/core/Rng.java.

Rng also provides the building blocks of resampling: bootstrapIndices(n) (nn indices with replacement), permutation(n) and shuffle (Fisher-Yates from the back: for i=n−1i = n-1 down to 1, aia_i is swapped with a random aja_j, 0≤j≤i0 \le j \le i) and sampleWithout(n, k) (the first kk of a permutation).

Worked example: the quantile t0.975;10t_{0.975;\,10}

A 95 % confidence interval from 11 measurements needs the 97.5 % quantile of the t distribution with 10 degrees of freedom.

  1. StudentT.quantile(0.975) calls bracketQuantile with the bracket [−10,10][-10, 10].
  2. f(−10)=F(−10)−0.975<0f(-10) = F(-10) - 0.975 < 0 and f(10)>0f(10) > 0: the bracket contains the root, no widening is needed.
  3. Brent searches for the root; each evaluation of FF is an incomplete beta function Iy(5,12)I_y(5, \frac12) with y=10/(10+t2)y = 10/(10+t^2).
  4. Result: 2.22813885198 (recomputed); R gives qt(0.975, 10) = 2.2281388519649385. Backwards, cdf(2.2281388519649385) = 0.974999999999.

Further recomputed values: χ0.95;32=7.81472790326\chi^2_{0.95;\,3} = 7.81472790326, F0.95;3,12=3.49029481950F_{0.95;\,3,12} = 3.49029481950, median of gamma(2, 1) =1.67834699002= 1.67834699002, P(X≤5)P(X \le 5) for binomial(10; 0.5) =0.6230468749999967= 0.6230468749999967 (the exact value is 638/1024=0.623046875638/1024 = 0.623046875).

  • Quantilprobe (31 checks) checks the shared foundation Dist: published normal quantiles to 10−910^{-9} relative; symmetry q(1−p)=−q(p)q(1-p) = -q(p) at 1999 points, in the middle to 10−1410^{-14}; strict monotonicity; round trip Φ(q(p))=p\Phi(q(p)) = p to 10−1210^{-12}; the branch boundaries at 0.02425; q(10−10)q(10^{-10}) to 10−1210^{-12} and the back-computation of q(10−20)q(10^{-20}). The Quantilprobe was sharpened with 19 counter-experiments (each a class with exactly one deliberate text change). Two of them improved the probe: a bracket search without downward widening returns NaN, and NaN slipped through the comparison abs(actual − expected) > tol. Since then the comparisons read !(abs(actual − expected) <= tol).

Limits

  • Quantiles by bracketing cost a few dozen evaluations of the incomplete beta or gamma function, depending on the distribution. Whoever needs many random numbers from t, chi-squared, F or beta pays this price per number.
  • Bracketing reaches an absolute accuracy of about 10−1210^{-12} in xx; for quantiles very close to 0 (for instance chi-squared with pp near 0) that is coarse in relative terms.
  • The hypergeometric distribution function sums point probabilities; for large populations this becomes slow, and in the upper tail FF is capped at 1.
  • The parameters are not checked (σ≤0\sigma \le 0, pp outside [0,1][0, 1], ν≤0\nu \le 0).
  • The t distribution only makes sense for ν>0\nu > 0; for ν≤1\nu \le 1 the mean is NaN, for 1<ν≤21 < \nu \le 2 the variance is infinite.
