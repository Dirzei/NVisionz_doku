Confidence, prediction and tolerance intervals – background

The computation behind Estimate: intervals for mean, variance and standard deviation, for median and quantiles from order statistics, four methods for proportions, prediction and tolerance interval of the normal distribution, bootstrap intervals as percentile and BCa.

What this is about

A mean of ten measurements is not the true mean but an estimate that would come out differently with the next series of measurements. An interval tells how far one can trust this estimate. Three kinds of interval are often confused although they answer quite different questions:

IntervalQuestionas nn grows it becomes
confidence intervalWhere is the true parameter (for instance μ\mu)?arbitrarily narrow
prediction intervalWhere will the next single measurement be?never narrower than the spread itself
tolerance intervalWhere does a given fraction (for instance 95 %) of all future values lie – with a given confidence?approaches μ±zσ\mu \pm z\sigma

A 95 % confidence interval means: in repeated samples the procedure yields an interval containing the true value in 95 % of cases. It does not mean that the true value lies in this interval with 95 % probability – the true value is fixed, the interval is random.

Framework convention: All intervals are two-sided and symmetric in the error probabilities: α/2\alpha/2 below, α/2\alpha/2 above, with α=1−level\alpha = 1 - \text{level}. The result is a ConfInt(lower, upper, level). There are no one-sided intervals.

Mean, variance, standard deviation

Mean

If the data are normally distributed, T=(x−μ)/(s/n)T = (\bar x - \mu)/(s/\sqrt n) is t distributed with n−1n-1 degrees of freedom. It follows that

x±t1−α/2;n−1sn\bar x \pm t_{1-\alpha/2;\,n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n}

Where the t distribution comes from: for normal data, x\bar x is normal with variance σ2/n\sigma^2/n, (n−1)s2/σ2(n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 is chi-squared with n−1n-1 degrees of freedom, and the two are independent (Cochran's theorem). The quotient of a standard normal number and the root of an independent chi-squared number divided by its degrees of freedom is t distributed by definition; σ\sigma cancels. One degree of freedom is lost because ss scatters around x\bar x instead of μ\mu. For 1…10, t0.975;9=2.2622t_{0.975;9} = 2.2622; with z=1.96z = 1.96 instead of tt the interval would be 13 % too narrow.

Owing to the central limit theorem this interval also holds approximately for non-normal data, as soon as nn is not too small and the distribution not too skewed.

Variance and standard deviation

For normally distributed data (n−1)s2/σ2(n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 is chi-squared distributed with n−1n-1 degrees of freedom. Solving for σ2\sigma^2 gives an asymmetric interval:

[(n−1)s2χ1−α/2;n−12,(n−1)s2χα/2;n−12]\left[\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2;\,n-1}},\ \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{\alpha/2;\,n-1}}\right]

The interval of the standard deviation consists of the square roots of the limits. Unlike the interval for the mean, this interval is not robust against departures from normality: even slightly heavy-tailed data make it clearly too narrow.

What follows from this. The reason for the sensitivity lies in the variance of s2s^2. In general, without assuming normality, with the excess kurtosis κ\kappa

Var⁡(s2)=σ4(2n−1+κn)\operatorname{Var}(s^2) = \sigma^4\left(\frac{2}{n-1} + \frac{\kappa}{n}\right)

The chi-squared formula assumes κ=0\kappa = 0. For a Laplace distribution (κ=3\kappa = 3) and n=10n = 10 the true variance of s2s^2 is 2.35 times as large as assumed, and the second term does not vanish relative to the first even for large nn: the interval never becomes right, it only becomes narrower. For the mean, by contrast, the variance of x\bar x depends only on σ2\sigma^2, and the central limit theorem applies.

Wilson as an inverted test

A z-test does not reject p0p_0 as long as (p^−p0)2≤z2p0(1−p0)/n(\hat p - p_0)^2 \le z^2 p_0(1-p_0)/n – with the variance under the null hypothesis, not the estimated one. The limits are the two solutions of the quadratic equation in p0p_0:

(1+z2n)p02−(2p^+z2n)p0+p^2=0\left(1 + \frac{z^2}{n}\right)p_0^2 - \left(2\hat p + \frac{z^2}{n}\right)p_0 + \hat p^2 = 0

The quadratic formula gives exactly the row of the table: centre (p^+z2/(2n))/(1+z2/n)(\hat p + z^2/(2n))/(1 + z^2/n), half width z1+z2/np^(1−p^)/n+z2/(4n2)\frac{z}{1+z^2/n}\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n + z^2/(4n^2)}. The centre is pulled towards 12\frac12; that is why Wilson does not degenerate to width zero at k=0k = 0. Agresti-Coull takes over this centre and puts a Wald width around it.

Clopper-Pearson as an inverted binomial test

The lower limit is the pp at which P(X≥k∣p)=α/2P(X \ge k \mid p) = \alpha/2, the upper one the pp with P(X≤k∣p)=α/2P(X \le k \mid p) = \alpha/2. By the relation between the binomial and beta distributions (see Distributions), P(X≥k∣p)=Ip(k,n−k+1)P(X \ge k \mid p) = I_p(k, n-k+1), a beta distribution function in pp; the equation is therefore solved by the beta quantile. Because the binomial distribution has steps, the coverage at every pp is at least 1−α1 - \alpha – hence “exact” and at the same time conservative.

The Wald interval is the textbook formula but does not keep its level for small nn or p^\hat p near 0 or 1: at k=0k = 0 its width is zero. Wilson inverts the test instead – it contains all p0p_0 that a z-test would not reject – and has a coverage close to the nominal one. Agresti-Coull is the simple approximation to it: “add two successes and two failures” (for z≈2z \approx 2). Clopper-Pearson is “exact”: it inverts the binomial test and uses the relation between the binomial and the beta distribution. Its coverage is always at least the nominal level, usually above – the interval is conservatively wide.

What follows from this. The actual coverage can be computed exactly for every pp: one sums the binomial probabilities of all kk whose interval contains pp. For n=10n = 10, nominal 95 %, over pp from 0.01 to 0.99:

Methodcoverage at p=0.8p = 0.8smalleston average

At n=30n = 30 the picture stays the same, the dips become shallower (Wald on average 0.889, Wilson 0.953). This agrees with Brown, Cai and DasGupta (2001).

Framework convention: Wald, Wilson and Agresti-Coull are clipped to [0,1][0, 1]. The choice is free when calling; the module recommends none, and the GUI catalogue does not offer proportion intervals.

Median and quantiles from order statistics

For a quantile ξp\xi_p there is a distribution-free interval: the number of values below ξp\xi_p is binomially distributed with (n,p)(n, p), whatever the distribution of the data. With the binomial quantiles

r=FBin(n,p)−1(α/2),s=FBin(n,p)−1(1−α/2)+1r = F^{-1}_{\text{Bin}(n,p)}(\alpha/2), \qquad s = F^{-1}_{\text{Bin}(n,p)}(1-\alpha/2) + 1

Derivation: x(r)≤ξpx_{(r)} \le \xi_p means “at least rr values lie below ξp\xi_p”, ξp<x(s)\xi_p < x_{(s)} means “at most s−1s-1”. With BB = number of values below ξp\xi_p, binomial with (n,p)(n, p) for a continuous distribution, P(x(r)≤ξp<x(s))=P(r≤B≤s−1)P(x_{(r)} \le \xi_p < x_{(s)}) = P(r \le B \le s-1). Choosing rr and ss via the binomial quantiles leaves at most α/2\alpha/2 in each tail. If ss is limited to nn (for quantiles near 1 and small nn), the upper limit is the maximum and the level falls short; the module does not report this.

[x(r),x(s)][x_{(r)}, x_{(s)}] is an interval with coverage P(r≤B≤s−1)≥1−αP(r \le B \le s-1) \ge 1-\alpha. The indices are limited to 1…n1 \dots n. medianCI is the case p=0.5p = 0.5.

s ← sorted copy;  B ← Binomial(n, p)
r ← B.quantile(α/2)
t ← B.quantile(1 − α/2) + 1
r ← clamp to [1, n];  t ← clamp to [1, n]
return [s[r], s[t]]                                  1-based

Prediction and tolerance interval

Prediction interval

A new measurement xn+1x_{n+1} deviates from the sample mean by two independent errors: its own spread σ2\sigma^2 and the uncertainty of x\bar x, σ2/n\sigma^2/n. Hence

x±t1−α/2;n−1s1+1n\bar x \pm t_{1-\alpha/2;\,n-1}\; s\sqrt{1 + \frac1n}

Because xn+1−xx_{n+1} - \bar x is normal with mean 0 and variance σ2+σ2/n\sigma^2 + \sigma^2/n, and independent of ss; divided by s1+1/ns\sqrt{1+1/n} it is t distributed with n−1n-1 degrees of freedom. For n→∞n \to \infty the width stays 2zσ2z\sigma – the scatter of the individual values does not vanish.

Tolerance interval

We seek x±ks\bar x \pm k s such that with confidence γ\gamma at least the fraction PP of the population lies inside. The exact factor kk exists only as the solution of an integral equation (below); Howe's approximation (1969) serves as start value:

k=z(1+P)/2(n−1)(1+1n)χ1−γ;n−12k = z_{(1+P)/2}\,\sqrt{\frac{(n-1)\left(1 + \frac1n\right)}{\chi^2_{1-\gamma;\,n-1}}}

with the lower (1−γ)(1-\gamma) quantile of the chi-squared distribution. The first factor would already be the answer for known μ\mu and σ\sigma; the root enlarges it for the uncertainty of x\bar x (1+1/n1 + 1/n) and of ss (chi-squared quantile).

The idea of the approximation: the share covered by x±ks\bar x \pm ks depends only weakly on x\bar x; Howe replaces the scatter of x\bar x by the factor 1+1/n1 + 1/n as for the prediction interval and inserts for ss the unfavourable limit s2≥σ2χ1−γ;n−12/(n−1)s^2 \ge \sigma^2 \chi^2_{1-\gamma;\,n-1}/(n-1), which holds with probability γ\gamma. For 1…10, k=3.3819k = 3.3819. The exact factor for n=10n = 10, P=γ=0.95P = \gamma = 0.95 is 3.3934; the approximation is 0.3 % too narrow, its confidence lies just below 95 % (for n=5n = 5, P=0.9P = 0.9, γ=0.99\gamma = 0.99 by 0.7 %). As nn grows the difference disappears.

The exact factor (Odeh 1978; Krishnamoorthy and Mathew 2009). For given x\bar x, x±ks\bar x \pm ks covers the fraction Φ(z+ks/σ)−Φ(z−ks/σ)\Phi(z + ks/\sigma) - \Phi(z - ks/\sigma), z=(x−μ)/σz = (\bar x - \mu)/\sigma; it reaches PP as soon as ks/σ≥r(z)ks/\sigma \ge r(z) with Φ(z+r)−Φ(z−r)=P\Phi(z + r) - \Phi(z - r) = P. Because νs2/σ2\nu s^2/\sigma^2 is chi-squared distributed independently of x\bar x and nz\sqrt n\,z is standard normal, the confidence is

γ(k)=2n2π∫0∞P(χν2>νr(z)2k2)e−nz2/2dz,ν=n−1\gamma(k) = 2\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty P\Bigl(\chi^2_\nu > \frac{\nu\,r(z)^2}{k^2}\Bigr)\,e^{-n z^2/2}\,dz, \qquad \nu = n - 1

and kk is the solution of γ(k)=γ\gamma(k) = \gamma. The module integrates by Gauss-Kronrod up to z=40/nz = 40/\sqrt n (beyond that the weight is below 10−30010^{-300}), finds r(z)r(z) by bracketed Newton and kk by the Illinois method starting from Howe's factor. Recomputed with scipy: n=5n = 5, 90 %/99 %: 6.6549; n=2n = 2, 95 %/95 %: 36.519 (Howe 38.28, here too wide); n=100n = 100, 95 %/99 %: 2.3572 – as in the tables of Krishnamoorthy and Mathew.

Bootstrap intervals

For many statistics – median, trimmed mean, correlation, coefficient of variation – there is no handy formula for the spread. The bootstrap (Efron 1979) replaces the unknown distribution by the sample itself: one draws nn values with replacement from the data BB times, computes the statistic θ^b*\hat\theta^*_b each time and looks at their distribution.

Percentile interval

[θ^(α/2)*,θ^(1−α/2)*]\left[\hat\theta^*_{(\alpha/2)},\ \hat\theta^*_{(1-\alpha/2)}\right]

the quantiles (type 7) of the BB bootstrap values. Simple, but biased if the distribution of θ^\hat\theta is skewed or θ^\hat\theta has a bias.

BCa: bias-corrected and accelerated

Efron (1987) corrects the percentile limits by two quantities:

  • Bias correction z0z_0: what fraction of the bootstrap values lies below the estimate? For an unbiased, symmetric estimator one half, and then z0=0z_0 = 0. z0=Φ−1(#{θ^b*<θ^}B)z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\#\{\hat\theta^*_b < \hat\theta\}}{B}\right)
  • Acceleration aa: how strongly does the standard deviation of θ^\hat\theta change with the true value? Estimated via the jackknife – θ^(i)\hat\theta_{(i)} is the statistic without the ii-th value, θ(⋅)\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} their mean: a=∑i(θ(⋅)−θ^(i))36(∑i(θ(⋅)−θ^(i))2)3/2a = \frac{\sum_i (\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^3}{6\left(\sum_i (\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^2\right)^{3/2}}

The corrected fractions are then

α1=Φ(z0+z0+zα/21−a(z0+zα/2)),α2=Φ(z0+z0+z1−α/21−a(z0+z1−α/2))\alpha_1 = \Phi\left(z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_{\alpha/2}}{1 - a(z_0 + z_{\alpha/2})}\right), \qquad \alpha_2 = \Phi\left(z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_{1-\alpha/2}}{1 - a(z_0 + z_{1-\alpha/2})}\right)

and the interval consists of the α1\alpha_1 and α2\alpha_2 quantiles of the bootstrap values. With z0=a=0z_0 = a = 0 the percentile interval results again.

Where the formula comes from: Efron assumes there is a monotone transformation ϕ\phi under which ϕ(θ^)\phi(\hat\theta) is normal with mean ϕ(θ)−z0σϕ\phi(\theta) - z_0\sigma_\phi and a standard deviation σϕ=1+aϕ(θ)\sigma_\phi = 1 + a\,\phi(\theta) that grows linearly with the parameter. Solving for θ\theta and inserting the bootstrap distribution for the unknown distribution of θ^\hat\theta yields the two shares α1,α2\alpha_1, \alpha_2 – without having to know ϕ\phi. z0z_0 measures the offset of the median of the bootstrap values against θ^\hat\theta, aa the skewness of the influences of single values; for the mean, aa is one sixth of the sample skewness divided by n\sqrt n.

What follows from this. For right-skewed data a>0a > 0, and BCa shifts both limits upwards: for {1,2,3,4,5,6,8,12,20,40}\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 20, 40\} with the mean as statistic, a=0.093a = 0.093; the upper end lies higher than for the percentile interval. The coverage error of each single limit falls like 1/n1/n for BCa, but only like 1/n1/\sqrt n for the percentile interval (Efron 1987). The number BB of draws only determines the Monte Carlo scatter of the limits; for interval limits the literature recommends B≥1000B \ge 1000, for BCa rather 2000.

Pseudocode – Estimate.bootstrapCI(x, stat, level, B, rng, bca)

How it is checked

EstimateTest (12 checks) with 1…10 and R values: interval for the mean to 10−610^{-6} (R t.test(1:10)$conf.int), variance interval to 10−610^{-6} and 10−510^{-5}; Wilson for 8 of 10 to 2⋅10−52\cdot10^{-5}, Clopper-Pearson to 10−610^{-6}. In addition properties: the median interval contains 5.5; the prediction interval is wider than the confidence interval; percentile and BCa intervals with fixed seed contain the mean.

TheorieAprobe (11 checks, package envisionz.stats.estimate) checks the derivations of this page: all intervals of 1…10 against recomputed values to 10−910^{-9} to 10−1210^{-12}, the standard deviation interval as the root of the variance interval, Wald and Agresti-Coull including truncation to [0,1][0, 1], Wilson as an inverted z-test and Clopper-Pearson as an inverted binomial test at the limits, the order statistics with limitation to nn, Howe's tolerance factor and the exact one at four points, and percentile and BCa against an independent recomputation with the same draws.

Limits

  • Variance, prediction and tolerance intervals hold only for normally distributed data and are sensitive to departures.
  • The tolerance interval is two-sided only; one-sided tolerance limits (via the noncentral t distribution) are missing.
  • Bootstrap intervals for very small samples (below about 15 values) are too narrow; BCa helps but does not cure. The median as a statistic has a very step-like bootstrap distribution for small nn.
  • The BCa acceleration costs nn extra evaluations of the statistic; if the statistic itself is expensive this matters for large nn.
  • No one-sided intervals, no intervals for differences of two proportions, no continuity correction for Wilson.
