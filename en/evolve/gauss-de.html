Numeric genes – Gaussian mutation and differential evolution

The computation behind NumericGenome, GaussianMutationProposer and DifferentialEvolutionProposer: how a genome of strings becomes a vector with bounds, how random mutation scatters, why differential evolution finds its step size by itself – with pseudocode following the source, worked examples, measurements, the checks and the limits. CMA-ES and the surrogate model are on the page CMA-ES and surrogate model.

What this is about

The most frequent search task in the framework is a setting made of numbers: thresholds, filter radii, weights, starting values of a solver. Every number has a sensible range, some are integers. The search takes place in an axis-parallel box

Ω=[l1,u1]×[l2,u2]×…×[ln,un]⊂Rn.\Omega = [l_1, u_1] \times [l_2, u_2] \times \dots \times [l_n, u_n] \subset \mathbb{R}^n.

An operator for such genes must answer two questions: in which direction should a child deviate from its parent, and how far? Gaussian mutation answers both with fixed settings – random direction, fixed step size. Differential evolution reads both off the population. That is the whole difference, and it shows clearly in the measurements.

From genome to vector: NumericGenome

A Candidate carries strings; the operators compute with vectors. NumericGenome mediates: it holds an ordered list of genes (ki,li,ui,integeri)(k_i, l_i, u_i, \text{integer}_i) and maps in both directions.

  • Genome → vector (toVector): every value is read as a number and clipped to [li,ui][l_i, u_i]. If it is missing or unreadable it becomes the midpoint (li+ui)/2(l_i+u_i)/2.
  • Vector → genome (toGenome): every value is clipped; integer genes are rounded to the nearest integer, all others to four decimal places. All other genes – enumerations, switches, expressions – are taken over unchanged from the base genome.
  • Normalising (normalize): zi=(xi−li)/(ui−li)z_i = (x_i - l_i)/(u_i - l_i), zero for a span below 10−1210^{-12}. The surrogate model measures distances in these units.

If no better bounds are available, inferFrom(sample, factor) derives them from a sample genome. Everything that can be read as a number becomes a numeric gene; it is an integer gene if the text consists only of digits with an optional minus. For a starting value vv and a factor ϕ\phi:

[l,u]={[−1,1],|v|<10−9,v>0,v<0.[l, u] = \begin{cases} [-1,\; 1], & |v| < 10^{-9} \\ [v/\phi,\; v\,\phi], & v > 0 \\ [v\,\phi,\; v/\phi], & v < 0. \end{cases}

Convention in the framework: Numeric operators touch genes only through NumericGenome. As a result they leave structural genes alone, and every value written back into the genome lies within the bounds. The rounding to four decimal places is absolute, not relative to the span. The derived bounds are coarse; an EvaluatorProvider that knows the real bounds supplies them via numericGenome().

Gaussian mutation

The simplest answer to "where to and how far": a parent pp is drawn uniformly from the given parents (without parents, from the best examples of the context). Then a number cc is chosen uniformly from {1,…,cmax⁡}\{1, \dots, c_{\max}\}, and cc times a gene ii is drawn uniformly and shifted:

xi←clamp[li,ui]⁡(xi+σf(ui−li)ξ),ξ∼N(0,1).x_i \leftarrow \mathrm{clamp}_{[l_i, u_i]}\!\left(x_i + \sigma_f\,(u_i - l_i)\,\xi\right), \qquad \xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).

The spread is thus a fixed share σf\sigma_f (sigmaFraction) of the respective span. The method does not adapt: if the solution is already accurate to 10−310^{-3} of the span, a child with σf=0.1\sigma_f = 0.1 typically jumps a hundred times too far.

Pseudocode – GaussianMutationProposer.propose(parents, context, n)

pool ← parents, if empty: context.topExamples     both empty → empty list
for k ← 1 to n:
    p ← pool[rnd.int(|pool|)].candidate
    x ← map.toVector(p.genes)
    c ← 1 + rnd.int(maxChanges)                       1 … maxChanges
    repeat c times:
        i ← rnd.int(number of genes)                   with replacement
        x[i] ← clamp_i(x[i] + rnd.gauss() · span_i · sigmaFraction)
    child ← p.child(map.toGenome(x, p.genes), MUTATION)

Source: envisionz-framework-evolve/src/envisionz/evolve/GaussianMutationProposer.java. The operator is stateless; maxChanges below 1 is set to 1, and the constructor rejects a genome without numeric genes.

Convention in the framework: Genes are drawn with replacement. The same gene may be shifted twice in one child; then fewer genes change than cc, and that one scatters with 2σf\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_f of the span. The operator's role is that of the baseline and the warm-up: it always delivers children, even with a single elite, and fills the archive until the more demanding operators have enough population.

Differential evolution

Differential evolution after Storn and Price draws three members a,b,ca, b, c from the population and forms the trial vector

v=a+F(b−c).v = a + F\,(b - c).

It is then crossed uniformly with the target vector tt – the member whose child is to be created: every component comes from vv with probability CRCR, otherwise from tt; one randomly chosen component jj comes from vv in any case, so that the child is never equal to the target:

ui={vi,i=j or ri<CRti,otherwise,ri∼U(0,1).u_i = \begin{cases} v_i, & i = j \text{ or } r_i < CR \\ t_i, & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \qquad r_i \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1).

Why it works. The difference of two members carries direction and scale that actually occur in the population. If the population is widely spread, the differences are large and the steps wide; if it contracts, the steps become small by themselves. If it lies along an oblique valley, the differences point along the valley. Nobody has to set a step size – FF only scales what the population dictates.

The variants. In DE/rand/1, aa is a random member; in DE/best/1 the best one. DE/best/1 converges faster but loses diversity, because all trial vectors lie around the same point.

Worked example. Population as in the figure, target t=(30,40)t = (30, 40), a=(0,0)a = (0,0), b=(70,90)b = (70, 90), c=(10,20)c = (10, 20), F=0.7F = 0.7:

v=(0,0)+0.7((70,90)−(10,20))=(42,49).v = (0, 0) + 0.7\,\big((70, 90) - (10, 20)\big) = (42,\ 49).

With two genes and CR=0.9CR = 0.9: if the forced component is xx (probability ½), yy also comes from vv with 0.9 – child (42,49)(42, 49) –, otherwise from tt – child (42,40)(42, 40). Symmetrically for yy. Together: P(u=v)=0.9P(u = v) = 0.9, P(u=(42,40))=P(u=(30,49))=0.05P(u = (42, 40)) = P(u = (30, 49)) = 0.05.

Pseudocode – DifferentialEvolutionProposer.propose(parents, context, n)

// population: the parents, then the context examples without duplicates
pop ← [toVector(e) for e in parents]
pop ← pop + [toVector(e) for e in context.topExamples,
                                 if e not already contained]
if |pop| < 4: return empty list                   too few for a difference
best ← pop[0]                                        the best according to the comment – see Limits
for k ← 1 to n:
    ti ← rnd.int(|pop|);  t ← pop[ti]
    ai ≠ ti, bi ∉ {ti, ai}, ci ∉ {ti, ai, bi}            each uniform, by repetition
    a ← if bestVariant then best else pop[ai]
    v ← clamp(a + F · (pop[bi] − pop[ci]))
    j ← rnd.int(n_genes)
    u ← t
    for i ← 0 to n_genes − 1:
        if i = j or rnd.uniform() < CR: u[i] ← v[i]
    child ← cand[ti].child(map.toGenome(u, cand[ti].genes), CROSSOVER)   cand: the candidates of pop

Source: envisionz-framework-evolve/src/envisionz/evolve/DifferentialEvolutionProposer.java. The operator is stateless and reads the population anew on every call; it needs no feedback.

Convention in the framework: The population is not the whole archive but the drawn parents (default two) plus the best examples of the context (default four) – at most six vectors. The trial vector is clipped to the bounds before crossover and once more when written back. The children carry the origin CROSSOVER and the target vector as parent. Usual values according to the class comment: FF between 0.5 and 0.9, CRCR between 0.7 and 0.9.

The sign of FF is not observable. Because bb and cc are drawn uniformly and in either order, the set of possible children is symmetric under F→−FF \to -F: for every pair (b,c)(b, c) the pair (c,b)(c, b) occurs with the same probability. With DE/rand/1 no check can find a flipped sign – it changes nothing.

How it is checked

Evolveprobe measures against anchors that can be computed in closed form, not against an earlier run:

  • Spread of the Gaussian mutation: a genome with one gene x∈[0,100]x \in [0, 100], start 50, σf=0.05\sigma_f = 0.05, 4000 children. The measured standard deviation must be within 5 % of 100⋅0.05=5100 \cdot 0.05 = 5 (measured 5.0420), the mean deviation within 5 % of 5 around zero. One gene, so that exactly one change falls per child.
  • Bounds: with a deliberately far too coarse spread (σf=5\sigma_f = 5) no child leaves [0,100][0, 100], and both bounds are also reached (below 1 and above 99) – otherwise the clipping would remain unchecked.
  • Number of changes: with five genes and maxChanges 3 never more than three, never zero, and three do occur.
  • Rule of differential evolution: four individuals, CR=1CR = 1 (the child is then vv), DE/best/1 with the first individual as aa: all possible results a+F(b−c)a + F(b - c) are computed, each of the 300 children must lie among them, and more than three distinct ones must occur. A wrong FF or a swapped difference would fall out at once.
  • Minimum population: with three individuals an empty list comes back.
  • Clipping in DE: with F=5F = 5 every child stays within the bounds.
  • Repeatability: the same seed gives the same children.

Limits

  • DE/best/1 takes the first parent, not the best. The code uses the first member of the population as the "best" vector and assumes that the list is sorted by fitness. The population, however, begins with the drawn parents, which are not sorted; only the context examples are. Recomputed with one gene, F=0.5F = 0.5, CR=1CR = 1: parents at x=40x = 40 (fitness −4) and x=30x = 30 (fitness −3), best examples at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40 with fitness 0 to −4. All 2000 children lay between 20 and 60, i.e. around the first parent at 40 – around the best example at 0 they would have had to lie between −20 and 20. The probe does not notice this, because it passes the population already sorted. DE/rand/1 is not affected.
  • At least four individuals. With fewer, DE delivers nothing. Used alone and with a single seed, the engine then runs endlessly (see Search run); Gaussian mutation in the mix prevents that.
  • Small population. At most six vectors with the defaults; with many parameters the differences span only a small subspace. The same elite may appear twice among the parents; then one difference is zero.
  • Gaussian mutation without adaptation. The step size is fixed; close to the optimum it becomes too large, far from it too small.
  • Rounding. Non-integer genes are rounded to 10−410^{-4} absolute. Parameters with spans of this order lose resolution; they should be scaled beforehand.
  • Integer genes with fractional bounds. Rounding happens after clipping. With derived bounds such as [1.3333;12][1.3333;\ 12] the value 1 can thus arise, which lies below the lower bound.
  • Bounds from inferFrom depend on the starting value: a parameter starting at 0 gets [−1,1][-1, 1], however large it sensibly becomes.
