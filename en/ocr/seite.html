Reading a page

The line recogniser reads one line. Before that someone has to find the lines — on a page with columns, images, frames and rules, without a given region. All measures follow from one quantity: the mean character height of the page.

Ink and areas

Every pixel is turned into its luminance; the threshold between ink and paper is chosen by Otsu’s method. It takes the threshold tt that maximises the between-class variance of dark and light:

σB2(t)=ω0(t)ω1(t)(μ0(t)−μ1(t))2,\sigma_B^2(t) = \omega_0(t)\,\omega_1(t)\,\big(\mu_0(t) - \mu_1(t)\big)^2 ,

with the shares ω0,ω1\omega_0, \omega_1 and the mean luminances μ0,μ1\mu_0, \mu_1 of the pixels below and above tt. So that an almost empty or almost black page does not get a meaningless threshold, it is clamped to 80 to 200. Connected ink pixels (8-neighbourhood) form areas.

The measure: mean character height

The mean character height hmh_m is the median of the area heights between 3 pixels and a tenth of the page height. The median, because images and frames are few but very large areas and i-dots are many small ones. With fewer than three areas of that size the page holds no text. All thresholds below are multiples of hmh_m; the same method therefore reads text 14 pixels high as well as 40.

Parts of a character

Some characters consist of several areas: the dot of the i, the lower loop of the g, the rings of the percent sign, a digit that falls apart at the threshold. Two areas are joined when they lie one above the other, overlap horizontally by at least half of the narrower one, one is lower than 0.85hm0.85\,h_m and the vertical gap is at most 0.25hm0.25\,h_m. Without this step the dot of the i would become a line of its own above its line.

Punctuation is not chained because a comma reaches below the baseline and therefore hardly overlaps its neighbours; it would otherwise become a piece of its own. It attaches to the line whose centre is vertically closest.

Line pieces

The remaining areas are processed from left to right. An area joins an open piece when it overlaps vertically by at least half with the range of the last two areas of the piece and starts at most 1.2hm1.2\,h_m to its right; among several candidates the smallest gap wins. Because only the last areas are compared, a piece follows a slightly skewed line too, and a wide column gap separates two pieces. Pieces flatter than 0.5hm0.5\,h_m are tick marks of an axis and are dropped.

Each piece is cut out with a margin above, below and at the sides; only the ink of its own areas is copied, the rest stays white. So no neighbouring line reaches into the margin, and the line recogniser sees the same kind of image as in training.

Blocks and reading order

Two pieces belong to the same block when they stand side by side in a line (half overlapping vertically, gap up to 3hm3\,h_m) or one below the other with overlapping width and a distance of up to one piece height. The blocks are the connected components of this relation. Within a block the lines run from top to bottom, the pieces of a line from left to right. Blocks are ordered by their top edge; if two start within two piece heights, the left edge decides. With two columns this gives the reading order column by column instead of line by line across both columns.

Reading, noise, skew

Before everything else the reader estimates the skew of the page and straightens it; a straight page is left untouched. Then it reads all line pieces on all cores. A piece that yields nothing, or only a single character with a confidence below 0.7, is noise — usually the rest of a frame line, read as I — and is dropped. The boxes refer to the straightened image; for display they are rotated back about the image centre.

Vertical text

For a region whose text may also stand vertically (an axis title) the reader reads unrotated and rotated by 90 and 270 degrees clockwise and takes the reading with the highest quality

g=s⋅min⁡(1,zn⋅4),g = \bar{s} \cdot \min\!\Big(1,\ \frac{z}{n \cdot 4}\Big),

with the mean confidence s\bar s of the nn lines read and zz characters in total; on a tie the unrotated one. The damping factor is needed: vertical text read unrotated falls apart into many lines of one or two characters, which are often read with fair confidence. Rotation is lossless, pixel by pixel.

Limits

  • Light text on a dark ground is not read: Otsu calls the dark part ink.
  • Text touching a rule becomes part of the large area and is dropped with it.
  • Text at a free angle is read only up to the skew of a whole page and at 90 and 270 degrees.
