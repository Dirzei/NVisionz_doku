TIFF — comparison against TIFF 6.0 and against Pillow

As of: 2026-09-07
Our implementation: envisionz.fileformats.tiff.TiffDatei with Tiffbild, Tiffeintrag, Tifflzw, Tiffpackbits, read-only, no third-party library
Specification: TIFF Revision 6.0 (Aldus/Adobe, 1992) and the TIFF Technical Notes — public; references give sections and field names
Compared against: Pillow 12.2.0 (Python)
Test bench: envisionz.fileformats.tiff.Tiffprobe — 3068 checks, thirty-four anchors, thirty-six counter-tests

The reference implementation. Pillow is the image library of the Python ecosystem, the 2010 fork of the original Python Imaging Library by Fredrik Lundh, started by Jeffrey "Alex" Clark and maintained today by the python-pillow team. For TIFF it is split in two: uncompressed files it reads and writes with its own code, while for the compressed schemes it has required libtiff since version 5.0.0 — the C library that grew out of Sam Leffler's work from 1988 and effectively defines the format. This comparison therefore measures against libtiff on the compressed paths and against Pillow itself on the uncompressed ones.

Why this one. Because TIFF has no normal case, only a set of combinations — and because libtiff, which sits behind Pillow's compressed paths, effectively defines the format. On the uncompressed paths one measures Pillow's own code, on the compressed ones libtiff itself. Together that is the most precise information obtainable without a vendor tool. That two findings against the reference product stand here has exactly this reason: they concern Pillow's own side, not libtiff.

Feature comparison

TIFF is the only format in this collection with no normal case. GIF, BMP and PNG have a usual form and a few side paths; here there is only the combination the writer chose. Strips or tiles, chunky or planar, II or MM, five compressions, six photometric interpretations, six bit depths, one image or twenty — and none of these choices is rare.

The name says it: Tagged Image File Format. A TIFF describes itself through fields, and that is why the format survived thirty years without changing. The price is in the same sentence: a reader that "reads TIFF" only ever reads the subset it was built for, and finds out at the next file.

A directory without an image

What was measured

Twenty-one foreign anchors: fifteen were written by Pillow 12.2.0, six were built by hand and read back by Pillow. With TIFF the foreign-written share is larger than with any other image format in this collection — Pillow writes all four common compressions, the predictor, strips of a chosen size, palettes, CMYK, sixteen bits and several directories by itself.

What it does not write is still half the traps: big-endian byte order, tiles, FillOrder = 2, photometric 0, a four-bit palette — and it writes planar storage wrongly (finding T1).

Five ways to the same pixels

Five anchors carry the same 45 pixels: uncompressed, LZW, LZW with predictor, PackBits and Deflate. That is the sharpest comparison there is for a decompressor — it needs no target numbers, only equality, and an error in exactly one method shows at once. The same arrangement again at size: an image of 48 × 32 exists uncompressed and as LZW, and there the code width grows repeatedly.

One counter-test that stayed green — and the bug the probe found

Of nineteen interventions eighteen went red. The green one was "compute the predictor byte-wise at sixteen bits", and the reason was again a gap in the fixture: no anchor carried both at once. lzwpraed has eight bits, grau16 no predictor. The branch in the reader that adds over samples rather than bytes at sixteen bits stood unproven. A twenty-first anchor — praed16, 12 × 6 at sixteen bits with predictor and LZW — closed it: 65 failures afterwards.

And the probe found a real bug, not just a gap. The CMYK-to-RGB conversion first stood as (65535 - c) * (65535 - k) / 65535 — computed in int. The intermediate result is nearly four thousand million and overflows an int; 235 became 255. The anchor cmyk showed it at once. It stands here because it is the kind of error that never shows without a foreign target value: an image that is too bright looks like an image.

What is hard about the format

The byte order is in the file. Two characters at the start, II or MM, and after that there is no place left where a number may be read without asking. Not in the header, not in the directory, not in a field value and not in the pixel data at sixteen bits. That is honest and it is expensive: every access to two or four bytes costs a case distinction, and a forgotten one yields a number off by a factor of 256 or 16.7 million.

The value is in the field — or it is not. Every field occupies twelve bytes, four of which are for the value. If it fits, it stands there directly; otherwise those four bytes are an offset. It depends on the size and not on the tag: a SHORT with two entries is inside, one with three is not. A reader that always takes the offset reads into the middle of the file for small images and gets plausible numbers; a reader that always takes the value mistakes an offset for the image width on large ones.

Tiles compute with the tile width, even at the edge. A tile row is always computed by the full tile width — including the tiles at the right edge that overhang the image and end in padding. Compute with the image width there and you read shifted from the second tile row on. The fixture is therefore 20 × 12 with a tile of 8 × 8: neither 20 nor 12 is divisible by eight.

Planar storage is no small thing. With PlanarConfiguration = 2 all the red values of the image come first, then all green, then all blue — in separate strips. The number of strips triples, and their order is plane-major, then row-major. A reader that ignores the field reads the red strip as chunky RGB and gets an image a third of the width, coloured wrongly three times over.

TIFF's LZW is not GIF's. The same algorithm, three differences in the same loop: TIFF packs most significant bit first, grows the code width one position earlier (the early change) and has a fixed initial width of nine. The early change is not in the 1992 specification — it came as a correction afterwards, because the widespread writers did it that way. On a small image the wrong rule does not show at all; the dictionary never gets past 511 entries.

The predictor runs over samples, not over bytes. Every value is a difference to its left neighbour in the same channel. At eight bits a sample is a byte, and computing back byte-wise happens to be right. At sixteen bits it is not — there the addition is sixteen bits wide, and a byte-wise reversal yields stripes. That exact place was covered by no fixture until 6 September 2026.

What is good: that a file says everything about itself. There is no field whose meaning depends on another without saying so; there is no length that must be guessed; and a reader that does not know a field can skip it, because type and count stand next to it. That is why TIFF has been unchanged since 1992 and still carries things nobody thought of in 1992. The price is that "reading TIFF" is not a finite task — it is a subset one chooses and writes down. That is exactly what this comparison is for.

The items

The Note column names the kind in front of the identifier. Only the gaps are work.

Our open gaps — this is the work

Deliberate decisions — nothing to do

T24 (decided) — No route through the general format detection

Datenquellen.oeffne rejects a TIFF and points at TiffDatei.lies. The same reason as with GIF (I15), BMP (B1) and PNG (P1): an image is not a table. With TIFF there is an extra wrinkle not present elsewhere: a file often contains several images, and a Datenquelle would have to say which one it means.

Decided by the owner on 2026-09-06. The item stood as a gap in five image formats with identical wording (BMP B1, GIF I15, PNG P1, TIFF T24, WebP W20). It is not one: a Datenquelle is a table view, and an image is not a table. A view with one row per pixel (x, y, red, green, blue) would be possible — at 4000x3000 that is twelve million rows, and what it would buy does not answer the question "what is in this file" but a different one. The readers exist and are named in the rejection message; that is the information the user needs. VTK (V9) and HDF4 (H8) are different: their content really is tabular, and there the view is being built.

T11 (decided) — A third LZW in the house

There were already two: envisionz.pdf.PdfFilters reads PDF's (the same bit direction as TIFF, with a switch for the early change) and envisionz.fileformats.gif.Giflzw reads GIF's. A shared decoder with switches would be the tidier home — but it would have to touch framework-pdf, an existing module. The owner decided on 6 September 2026: keep them separate. The reasoning holds: a decoder with three switches used by three modules is one place where a bug breaks three formats at once. Three small ones, each with its own probe, are cheaper here than one with three modes. The cross-reference stands in the class comment of Tifflzw.

T23 (decided) — Sixteen bits are carried in full

punkte() delivers eight-bit ARGB; at a bit depth of sixteen, punkte16() additionally delivers the full samples, and at eight bits it deliberately returns null instead of extrapolated values. The same arrangement as with PNG (P7), for the same reason: an invented number would look like one from the file. The way from sixteen to eight is rounded, not the high byte.

T25 (decided) — Writing

Pillow writes TIFF and supplied fifteen of the twenty-one foreign anchors. This module reads. The six hand-built anchors are test equipment, not an output path.

Properties of the format

T26 (property) — A directory without an image — new on 2026-09-07

A TIFF directory is a field list. An image is a directory plus pixels — and the side data embedded in other formats is the same directory without them: EXIF in WebP, APP1 in JPEG, eXIf in PNG. All three are a complete TIFF stream beginning with II or MM and never containing an image.

TiffDatei.lies rejects such a thing, and that is right: ImageWidth and ImageLength are mandatory in an image file, and a reader that lets them be missing goes on to read pixels that are not there. So the field reading was extracted as Tiffverzeichnis; TiffDatei.nebenkette delivers the same directories without claiming an image stands behind them, and Tiffbild passes its four reading methods through to it. One reader instead of two — the format's traps are described in one place and checked in one place.

The sub-directory does not hang in the chain. ExifIFD (34665) and GPSInfo (34853) stand as an offset on a field, not as the next link. Whoever follows only the chain sees a camera image's maker, model and orientation — and nothing of exposure time, aperture, sensitivity or focal length, because those all live in the sub-directory. Both are followed.

Along with that, the tags that actually occur in a camera file have got names — thirty of them, not the three hundred plus of the full EXIF list. A tag without a name is no information: 274 = [6] tells nobody anything, Orientation = [6] does. And a rational is printed as a fraction and not as a pair of numbers — an exposure time is one four-hundredth of a second and not "1 and 250".

The first user is the WebP reader (W4). The same route is open in JPEG and in PNG.

T16 (property) — CCITT — the fax coding — closed on 2026-09-07

Closed. Compressions 2 (plain run lengths), 3 (group 3, one- and two-dimensional) and 4 (group 4) are decoded. This was the largest single gap of this reader, and it was worth it: scanned documents are the most common TIFF content there is, and they are almost always group 4. Six anchors, written and read by Pillow 12.3.0 — forty by thirty-two in all three compressions, plus 1800 points wide, one- and two-dimensional. All six agree pixel for pixel.

Why there are two Huffman tables and not one. White runs on a sheet are on average much longer than black ones, so they get their own, shorter codes. Which table applies depends on which colour is due — and the two tables overlap. A reader consulting the wrong one still finds a valid code. There is no error message at that point, only a different image.

The three traps, each of which forced an anchor of its own. First the shared makeup codes 1792 to 2560: they apply to both colours. Whoever puts them only in the white table reads narrow images correctly and wide ones half — and 1800 points at 300 dpi is an A4 sheet, not a special case. The mutation for it stayed green on the first five anchors, because a two-dimensionally coded image takes long runs from the line above instead of writing them as a number; only a one-dimensional anchor with entirely black and entirely white lines turns it red (53 checks). Second the single bit after every EOL in group 3: it says whether the next line is coded one- or two-dimensionally. One bit — miss it and every code from there on is shifted by one place (42 red). Third the byte boundary in compression 2 and with T4Options bit 2 (30 red).

And two that sit in the method itself. The reference position b1 must be a change to the opposite colour; whoever does not check the parity takes the next best one (30 red). And pass mode moves to b2 and not to b1 — one change further (29 red). Five mutations, all red.

What is not built: the uncompressed mode permitted by T6Options bit 1. It does not occur in the wild — no widespread writer sets the bit — and it is reported if the bit is there after all. No foreign anchor for it could be produced: neither Pillow nor libtiff writes it.

T17 (property) — JPEG in TIFF — closed on 2026-09-07

Closed. A strip with compression 7 is a JPEG stream; it is handed to this project's own JPEG reader. When the gap was written down that sounded like passing bytes along, and in exactly one place it is not.

JPEGTables. The quantisation and Huffman tables may sit once in tag 347 instead of in every strip. A strip is then an abbreviated stream: SOI, SOF0, SOS, image data — and no JPEG reader in the world can read that alone. The two pieces must be joined: the trailing EOI falls off the tables, the leading SOI off the strip. That is exactly how libtiff writes, and therefore Pillow too. The mutation "ignore the tables" turns 43 checks red.

And the colour comes from the JPEG. With PhotometricInterpretation = 2 libtiff writes the components as red, green and blue and says so in the frame header through the component ids 'R', 'G', 'B'. Applying the YCbCr computation anyway yields a colour at every point — just the wrong one, and the image looks like an image (52 red). The JPEG reader therefore now also evaluates Adobe's APP14 marker: its eleventh byte says 0 (no transform), 1 (YCbCr) or 2 (YCCK). With PhotometricInterpretation = 6 on the other hand the JPEG reader already converts, and the TIFF conversion must not be laid on top.

Two anchors, because one would have measured nothing. The first (jpegImTiff) has the tables inside the strip and photometric 6; the second (jpegTafeln) has them shared and photometric 2. Each of the two mutations above stays green on the other anchor. Because JPEG is lossy, here — as in the JPEG comparison and for the same reason — a rounding distance of three counts per channel applies and not pixel equality. Compression 6, the withdrawn old variant, is sent down the same path; there is no anchor for it because no present-day writer still produces it.

T21 (property) — Photometric 6 — YCbCr — closed on 2026-09-07

Closed. The conversion is in TIFF6 section 21 and hangs on two fields that both have defaults and both like to be missing: YCbCrCoefficients (default 0.299 / 0.587 / 0.114 — CCIR 601) and ReferenceBlackWhite (default 0/255, 128/255, 128/255). Both are read.

The trap is green. It is not computed from Y, Cb and Cr but back out of red and blue — and from the unclamped values at that. Whoever clamps red and blue first and computes green afterwards gets a different green exactly where a colour is extreme anyway; and because that only happens at saturated places, an image read that way looks right. The anchor is built for it: Cb sits at zero, blue computes to −226 and is clamped, and green comes out as 0 instead of 44 in the wrong order (8 checks red).

And YCbCrCoefficients is a fraction. The field type is RATIONAL: numerator and denominator, eight bytes. Taking only the numerator reads 299 instead of 0.299. The first anchor did not carry the field at all — the default applied, and the mutation stayed green. It then measures not the reader but the anchor's reach. The anchor now carries it explicitly, with the same numbers as the default, and the same mutation turns 29 checks red.

What is not built: subsampling with uncompressed data. A YCbCrSubSampling of 2,2 changes the entire layout under compression 1: units of four Y plus one Cb and one Cr each, instead of pixel by pixel. On the JPEG path the JPEG reader does it, and there it is measured; uncompressed it is reported and not guessed. No foreign anchor could be produced for it — tifffile explicitly refuses to write it (cannot apply subsampling), and Pillow does not read an uncompressed YCbCr file at all (image file is truncated). Without a foreign reference value any implementation would merely be an assertion.

T4 (property) — BigTIFF — closed on 2026-09-07

Closed. Magic 43 is no longer rejected but read. BigTIFF differs in four places: the header is sixteen instead of eight bytes (after the 43 come the offset width 8 and a zero), the field count in the directory is eight instead of two bytes, a field is twenty instead of twelve bytes, and the value sits in the field if it fits in eight bytes instead of four. On top of that come field types 16, 17 and 18 (LONG8, SLONG8, IFD8).

Why letting the magic number through is not enough. Whoever does that reads a field count that is usually right — it sits in the lower two bytes of an eight-byte number — and nonsense afterwards. The two mutations hit exactly that: twelve bytes per field instead of twenty makes the very first directory fall apart, and four bytes of room in the field instead of eight turns 53 checks red. The anchor comes from tifffile 2025.5.10 and is deliberately small: the directory is meant to go wrong, not the image.

What remains is the message. A file with magic 43 whose offset width is not 8, or which lacks the zero behind it, is not a BigTIFF but broken — and the caller learns what is broken about it. The reference product does not read BigTIFF; its column stands at –.

T15 (property) — Predictor 3 — floating point — closed on 2026-09-07

Closed. The ordinary predictor differences neighbouring samples. With floating-point numbers that gains nothing: two neighbouring floats differ in almost all their bytes even when the numbers are close — sign, exponent and the upper mantissa bits are equal, the lower ones are noise. Predictor 3 therefore first splits the row by byte position and differences bytewise afterwards.

The order when reading is the trap. First accumulate the differences, then reassemble the byte positions. The other way round yields numbers that look like numbers: they are in range, they even mostly increase, and only the values themselves are wrong (15 checks red). And the reassembled bytes stand most significant first, independent of the file's byte order — with II they must be reversed afterwards (another 15 red). Anchor from tifffile 2025.5.10; the values are checked and not only the sum.

T10 (property) — Orientation — applied on request — closed on 2026-09-07

Closed, and with two methods rather than one. punkte() returns the pixels as they stand in the file — right for file inspection: whoever wants to know what is in a file does not want to see the result of a rotation. punkteAusgerichtet() applies tag 274 — for a viewer, because Orientation is an instruction and not a description. Both in one method does not work, and a default that sometimes rotates would be the worst of all answers.

With values 5 to 8 width and height swap places. Forgetting that yields an array of the right length with shifted rows — and that looks like a read error, not like a forgotten rotation. The mutation for it turns exactly one check red, and that is deliberate: it is sharp and hits only this one thing.

The anchor is measured against two foreign readers, and that is the point here: tifffile 2025.5.10 does not rotate, Pillow 12.3.0 does. A single reference value would leave open whether we never rotate or always. In addition, on a file without tag 274, both paths must be shown to give the same result.

T3 (property) — The byte order is in the file

See section 3. Two anchors therefore stand side by side: roh as II and grossbyte as MM, with the same pixels. They must come out pixel-identical — and the intervention "always read the byte order as II" makes the probe abort after 1909 checks.

T5 (property) — The value inside the field or behind it

See section 3. It is checked on a real anchor: ImageWidth fits in four bytes and is inside, BitsPerSample for truecolour needs six and is behind — both must come out right. Plus the boundary in closed form: SHORT times two is four bytes (inside), times three is six (behind).

T6 (property) — One file, several images

A TIFF is a chain of directories, and each describes a complete image — with its own size, bit depth and compression. A multi-page scan is not an exception but the format's normal case. Read only the first and a twenty-page fax gives you page one and no message.

T7 (property) — The chain can point in a circle

Nothing in the format stops a directory from pointing back at itself or at an earlier one. A reader without a check then runs forever — not wrongly, but not at all. We remember the offsets visited and abort with a finding. The intervention "drop the circular check" stops the probe from finishing at all; that is the sharpest answer a counter-test can give.

T8 (property) — Tiles compute with the tile width

See section 3. The anchor is 20 × 12 with a tile of 8 × 8, and the edge tiles at the right and bottom are checked expressly.

T9 (property) — FillOrder = 2

The bits are reversed within the byte — least significant first. The field is rare and easily overlooked; a reader that does not know it gets an image that looks like an image: every group of eight pixels is mirrored within itself. Pillow does not write it, so the anchor is hand-built.

T12 (property) — PackBits reads the control byte signed

0 to 127 means "the next n+1 bytes stand unchanged", −1 to −127 means "the next byte repeats 1−n times", −128 means nothing. Read the control byte unsigned and every repeat run looks like a long literal run, reading far past the end.

T13 (property) — The predictor runs over samples

See section 3 and 2.2. At eight bits a byte-wise reversal does not show; at sixteen it does. That place was covered by no fixture until 6 September 2026 and is covered since the anchor praed16.

T18 (property) — White is zero

Photometric 0 inverts the greyscale. It comes from fax machines and is the normal case for scanned documents. A reader confusing it with photometric 1 delivers a negative — with the same number of pixels, the same size and no message. Pillow does not write it, so the anchor is hand-built.

T19 (property) — ColorMap is stored channel-wise

First all the reds, then all the greens, then all the blues — not triple by triple. Read them as triples and you get a table in which every colour consists of three consecutive red values: an image in greys with a cast. The corresponding intervention, at 259 failures, is one of the sharpest in this probe.

T20 (property) — CMYK is multiplicative

The obvious formula would be 255 − (c + k); that is wrong, because two layers of ink do not add but act in sequence: what the first lets through, the second takes its share of. The correct form is (1 − c) · (1 − k). At c = 50 and k = 20 the additive computation gives 185, the multiplicative one 189 — and 189 is what the reference product delivers. A real bug arose here during construction and was found by the probe, see 2.2.

T22 (property) — Missing fields have defaults

Without BitsPerSample it is one, without SamplesPerPixel also one, without Compression it is 1 (uncompressed), without RowsPerStrip the whole image lies in one strip. Writers like to omit these fields on black-and-white images — Pillow does so for the anchor einbit — and a reader that presumes them falls over right there. The intervention "default BitsPerSample to eight" costs 140 checks.

Findings against the reference implementation

T1 (BUG there) — Planar storage is written wrongly

Ask Pillow to write a TIFF with PlanarConfiguration = 2 and it sets the field to 2 while storing the pixels chunky anyway. The file is then self-contradictory: the field says planar, the data is not. Pillow's own reader honours the field — so it reads its own file back wrongly and delivers an image with the colour channels jumbled. The bug is silent: there is no message on write, and on read one sees an image. That is why the anchor ebenen is hand-built.

How to reproduce — Create an RGB image of 4 x 2 with Image.new("RGB", (4,2)) and pixels (x*10, 100+x, 200+y) and write it with im.save(file, "TIFF", tiffinfo={284: 2}). Then look at the strip's bytes. Expected: all reds first, then all greens, then all blues. Observed with Pillow 12.2.0 under Python 3.13: the bytes are chunky — 0, 100, 200, 10, 101, 200, … — while tag_v2[284] reports 2. Read the same file back with Image.open and you get (0,0,0), (100,0,0), (200,0,0), …: Pillow reads its own file wrongly.

T2 (open there) — The ColorMap does not reach full scale

When writing a palette Pillow multiplies every eight-bit value by 256 instead of 257. The table then reaches 65280 and not 65535 — 255 becomes not white but 99.6 per cent white. On reading back Pillow takes the high byte again and recovers its value; whoever computes the value to eight bits by the book lands one below on about half the entries. It is not a bug — the specification only says the values are sixteen bits — but it is why the two palette anchors in this probe carry a tolerance of one. It is the same difference as between bit replication and rounding with BMP (B5), only on the writing side.

T14 (open there) — Four conditions without a statement about the file

A directory offset pointing past the end of the file; a strip offset doing the same; an unknown compression; an unknown field type. We report all four with tag and offset and read on as far as possible — the header is there, the dimensions are there, the pixels are missing. Pillow throws in these cases or silently delivers an empty image. That is not a bug in Pillow: it is an imaging library, not a file-inspection tool. With TIFF the difference weighs more than elsewhere, because the format has so many places where a file can be half broken.

The Java implementation — where this reader stands among its own kind

Why against javax.imageio in particular. Every other comparison in this module measures against a tool in a different language — Pillow, astropy, pyarrow, meshio. That is good against misunderstandings of the format and says nothing about where this reader stands among its own kind. javax.imageio is the Java implementation it actually competes with in the field: it sits on every machine this module runs on, and it costs no foreign library — the module's rule stays untouched. It is measured on every run, not once: Bildvergleichsprobe, 80 checks now that JPEG is included. And the reader measured against here really is pure Java: com.sun.imageio.plugins.tiff.TIFFImageReader has not a single native method. Measured on 2026-09-06, because adding JPEG revealed that this does not hold there — the JDK's JPEG reader is a JNI wrapper around libjpeg 6b. For this format, then, the comparison really is one among its own kind.

21 anchors, 17 pixel-identical. Raw, LZW with and without predictor, PackBits, Deflate, tiles, strips, planar, both byte orders, fill order 2, multi-page, palette, one bit, white-is-zero — all identical with javax.imageio.

The four remaining ones, each for a named reason. tafel and vierbittafel: a TIFF colour map carries sixteen bits per primary; we round when reducing to eight bits, ImageIO truncates — 0x2100 becomes 33 for us, 32 there. Our mapping hits the endpoints exactly, yet neither is wrong. cmyk: the file holds CMYK, and what RGB comes out of it is a decision and not a statement of the file — there is no right answer here. praed16 is the one finding against the reference implementation: sixteen bits with a horizontal predictor, and javax.imageio refuses with "16-bit samples are not supported for Horizontal differencing Predictor". We read it.

And a trap that is half the yield of the measurement. BufferedImage.getRGB() returns not the pixel from the file but its conversion to sRGB. For greyscale, ImageIO stores the values in a linear grey space and getRGB applies the sRGB curve on top: the raw value 0x25 becomes 0x6A — to three digits exactly (37/255)^(1/2.4) in sRGB encoding. The first run therefore reported differences on every greyscale image, in PNG and in BMP, that is on two independent readers of ours with the same symptom. It looked like a real bug and was none. The comparison goes through the raster. With TYPE_USHORT_GRAY the raster carries the raw 16-bit value while our punkte() is the view scaled to eight bits — whoever shifts there instead of rounding is off by one, and takes that for a bug too.
