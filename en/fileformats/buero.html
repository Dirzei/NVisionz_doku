Office files: slide order, dimensions and colour computation – background

Where the order of the slides comes from, in which units a presentation measures, and how a theme colour with its modifiers turns into the colour shown on the slide.

What it is about

A PowerPoint presentation is a package of XML parts (see Containers). A reader taking text and shape layout from it must get three things right: find the slides in presentation order, convert dimensions from the unit of the format, and compute the colours, which rarely stand as a number but as a theme colour with modifiers such as "75 % luminance".

The order of the slides

The slide number is not in the file name. The presentation order is in the list p:sldIdLst; each entry points to a part through a relationship id. Counting slide1.xml, slide2.xml, … is already wrong at ten slides (slide10 sorts before slide2) and entirely wrong for any reordered presentation.

Dimensions in EMU

DrawingML measures lengths in whole English Metric Units. The unit is chosen so that inches and centimetres both divide evenly:

1inch=914400EMU,1pt=91440072=12700EMU,1cm=9144002.54=360000EMU.1\ \text{inch} = 914\,400\ \text{EMU}, \qquad 1\ \text{pt} = \frac{914\,400}{72} = 12\,700\ \text{EMU}, \qquad 1\ \text{cm} = \frac{914\,400}{2.54} = 360\,000\ \text{EMU} .(1)

A 16 : 9 slide is 12,192,000 × 6,858,000 EMU, i.e. 960 × 540 pt. Percentages are stored in thousandths of a percent: 75 % is val="75000".

Luminance and saturation in HLS space

The modifiers lumMod, lumOff, satMod and satOff act on luminance LL and saturation SS of the HLS model. With M=max⁡(r,g,b)M = \max(r, g, b) and m=min⁡(r,g,b)m = \min(r, g, b)

L=M+m2,S={M−mM+mL≤0.5M−m2−M−mL>0.5L = \frac{M + m}{2}, \qquad S = \begin{cases} \dfrac{M - m}{M + m} & L \le 0.5 \\ \dfrac{M - m}{2 - M - m} & L > 0.5 \end{cases}(2)

and the hue HH depends on which channel is the maximum. The modifier computes L'=min⁡(1,max⁡(0,L⋅lumMod+lumOff))L' = \min(1, \max(0, L \cdot \text{lumMod} + \text{lumOff})), likewise for SS, and converts back to RGB. This yields the steps of the colour picker: for theme colour 4472C4, "darker 25 %" (lumMod 75 %) gives 2F5597, "lighter 40 %" (lumMod 60 %, lumOff 40 %) gives 8FAADC.

Tint and shade in linear RGB

tint mixes with white, shade with black – not in the stored sRGB values but in linear intensities. With the sRGB transfer curve

clin={c/12.92c≤0.04045(c+0.0551.055)2.4otherwisec_{\text{lin}} = \begin{cases} c/12.92 & c \le 0.04045 \\ \left(\frac{c + 0.055}{1.055}\right)^{2.4} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}(3)

we have tint(c)=sRGB(1−(1−clin)t)\text{tint}(c) = \text{sRGB}(1 - (1 - c_{\text{lin}})\,t) and shade(c)=sRGB(clins)\text{shade}(c) = \text{sRGB}(c_{\text{lin}}\,s). The difference is visible: tint 40 % on 4472C4 gives CFD5EA linearly, but B4C7E7 when computed on the sRGB values. scrgbClr also states its components linearly and passes through the same curve.

Base values and opacity

A colour can be given as a hex value (srgbClr), as a system colour with its last seen value (sysClr, otherwise window white and everything else black), as a named colour (prstClr), as a theme colour (schemeClr) or as the style placeholder (phClr). The opacity alpha is clamped to [0, 1].

What follows

  • Assumption: modifiers are applied in file order, each to the result of the previous one.
  • Computation is in floating point, rounded to whole bytes at the end; the colour-picker steps come out exactly.
  • A hex value that is not one yields no colour rather than a guessed one.

How it is checked

  • pptx.lage.TheorieFEprobe: the five luminance steps of the colour picker for 4472C4, saturation, all three hue branches, tint and shade, scrgbClr, system colours, named colours, opacity.
  • Pptxprobe, Pptxrestprobe, Gestaltprobe: slide order, colour mapping, backgrounds and shapes on third-party anchors.
  • Recalculation: docs/theorie/rechnung/fileformats_buero.py with an HLS implementation independent of the reader.

Limits

  • hueMod, comp, inv and gray are not computed.
  • Of the named colours the reader knows only the most common; an unknown one yields no colour.
  • Old binary formats (PPT, DOC) deliver text only, no layout and no colours.
