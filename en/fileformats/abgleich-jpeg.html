JPEG — comparison against ITU-T T.81 and against Pillow

As of: 2026-09-27
Our implementation: envisionz.fileformats.jpeg.JpegDatei with Jpegleser, read-only, no third-party library
Specification: ITU-T T.81 (ISO/IEC 10918-1, 1992) for the method and ITU-T T.871 (JFIF) for the container — both public; references give sections and marker names
Compared against: Pillow 12.2.0 (Python, via libjpeg-turbo 2.1.5)
Test bench: envisionz.fileformats.jpeg.Jpegprobe — 80 checks, fourteen foreign anchors, twenty-four counter-tests; Progressivprobe — 24 checks, ten counter-tests; Jpegvarianteprobe — 46 checks, nineteen foreign anchors from libjpeg-turbo 3.1.3, twelve counter-tests; Hierarchischprobe — 47 checks, ten foreign anchors from the T.873 reference software, six counter-tests

The reference implementation. The measurement went through Pillow, but it measured against libjpeg. Pillow's JPEG plugin binds libjpeg-turbo, and libjpeg-turbo is the maintained continuation of the Independent JPEG Group library — the implementation that has played the role of reference alongside the paper standard since 1991. It sits in browsers, in cameras, in Java, in Python. Whoever looks at a JPEG today looks at it with this code.

Why this one. Because JPEG is the only format in this collection where two correct readers may produce different numbers. The inverse transform is not specified bit-exactly in T.81, only bounded in accuracy; the reconstruction of subsampled chroma is not specified at all. A comparison demanding equality measures the wrong thing here. What is compared instead is whether the distance to the source formula — the one the image was generated from — is no larger than libjpeg's. That is the only question a lossy format actually answers.

Feature comparison

JPEG is not a format but a family. T.81 describes four methods — sequential, progressive, lossless and hierarchical — each optionally with Huffman or arithmetic coding, and the marker in the frame header says which one is present. What everyday speech calls "a JPEG" is the baseline method: sequential, Huffman, eight bits. This reader reads exactly that, since 2026-09-23 also the progressive method (J14), and rejects the others by name — not because they are hard, but because a half-read image looks like an image.

What was measured

Against libjpeg, not against a guessed bound

For every other format in this collection the check reads: equal or not equal. Here that does not work. T.81 does not specify the inverse transform bit-exactly — it was once bounded only in its accuracy, in a separate document (T.83) — and how a subsampled chroma component returns to full size is not specified anywhere at all. Two correct readers may therefore differ, and do.

The first version of the test therefore demanded "mean error against the source below 24". That was a guessed number, and it failed on two anchors: at quality 10 even libjpeg loses 28 counts, and on a large image with much structure 24. The loss depends on the quality and on the image, not on the decoder — so a fixed bound measures the wrong thing. What is demanded now is: no more than libjpeg, plus one count of slack for rounding. The reference figure is carried with every anchor, and it is measured, not decreed.

Alongside it stands the pixel-by-pixel comparison against libjpeg itself, at four places in every anchor. It is the sharper check, and it is the reason the reconstruction became correct at all: with nearest-neighbour the image deviated by 66 counts at 4:2:0 and by 60 at 4:2:2 — while 4:4:4 and greyscale sat at 2 and 1, that is within the rounding noise. A difference that appears only with subsampling and is large there is not rounding.

A counter-test that stayed green — and why it was discarded

Twenty counter-tests, nineteen of them red. The one that stayed green did not, following the house rule, call for another anchor but first for a calculation: under what condition does the intervention produce a different number at all?

The intervention rounded the sampling plane up to eight instead of to whole MCUs. The calculation says: never. The reader carries the plane width throughout as a stride — writing goes to zy · fBreite + zx, reading to cy · fBreite + cx, both sides use the same number. Make it larger and the plane merely sits more sparsely in memory; make it smaller and padding falls off on the right that nobody reads anyway, because reading only goes up to the real component size, and that is never larger than a width rounded up to eight. At 17×13 in 4:2:0 the luma plane becomes 24 wide instead of 32 — and reading goes to 16.

There is therefore no image that could turn this counter-test red. A sharper test would have to query the internal quantity itself and would thereby fix an implementation rather than a behaviour. The counter-test is accordingly discarded and not counted, with the calculation as its justification in the script; the line in the reader stays as a promise to the format and is marked as such. Incidentally the comment at that place was wrong — it claimed that rounding to eight would be off. It was corrected the same day.

Four errors that only the comparison found

(1) Two and four components ran into nothing. The frame header allowed 1 to 4 components — as the paper says — but the colour computation afterwards always reached for components 0, 1 and 2. With two components it ran past the end of the array; with four it read the first three as YCbCr and left the fourth lying: a wrong image with no message at all, and precisely for the CMYK files that come out of prepress. Both are now rejected by name. This surfaced while writing section 1.5 — not while testing.

(2) The supplied line count was half built. The reader accepted DNL and set the height if it was zero — except DNL comes after the scan, and the planes are allocated before it. With height zero a plane of height zero was created, the decoder wrote into nothing, and the first pixel produced a bare array index error. A good intention is not an implementation. Height zero is now rejected by name, and DNL stands as an open gap rather than a silent trap.

(3) The comment at the reconstruction said the opposite of the code. It claimed the nearest value was taken, and that a smoothed computation would be prettier but no longer match what the encoder put in. Both are wrong: the computation is triangular, and the smoothing is not the prettier but the more correct answer. The comment came from the first version and survived the rewrite.

(4) The comment at the sampling plane claimed an effect that does not exist — see 2.2. All four are comment or edge errors; none of them would have made an image visibly wrong. That is exactly why they stand here: they are the kind a test does not find, because nobody checks them.

What is hard about the format

Byte stuffing is the place where everything that follows turns to noise. No FF may appear in the Huffman stream that looks like a marker — so the encoder writes a 00 after every real FF. Read the 00 as data and you shift by eight bits, and from there every following code is wrong. There is no error message: badly decoded Huffman codes are still valid Huffman codes. The image starts cleanly and falls apart.

The zigzag applies twice, and one of the two is forgotten. The coefficients are in zigzag order — everyone knows that. But the quantisation table is in zigzag order too, and reading it in row order multiplies every coefficient by the wrong factor. The result is not a collapse but an image that almost works — same size, same edges, a visibly wrong contrast. It is the kind of error one looks at and takes for compression.

Lossy means there is no right answer, only a defensible one. With PNG, GIF or BMP a reader is correct when its bytes are correct. Here there are no bytes to check against — only a formula whose accuracy is bounded, and a reconstruction that is not specified at all. The entire arrangement of the test follows from this: a bound must be measured, not guessed, and the only meaningful reference is the loss the most widely used decoder has on the same file.

The reconstruction of colour is the single largest difference — and it is in no document. At 4:2:0 there is one chroma value for four pixels. How the other three come about is the reader's decision. Nearest neighbour is the obvious choice and is off by up to 66 counts against libjpeg — more than any other error this work found, and invisible nonetheless as long as one only checks against oneself. libjpeg computes triangularly: the new point is three quarters origin and one quarter neighbour, separately in each direction, together 9:3:3:1.

What is good: the marker structure. Every segment except the entropy stream states its length, and a reader that does not know a marker can skip it without knowing its layout. That is why JPEG files have carried Exif, ICC profiles and thumbnails around for thirty years without a single reader breaking on them — and why this reader can cleanly name the family members it does not read instead of guessing: the marker in the frame header tells it before a single bit of image data has been read.

The items

The Note column names the kind in front of the identifier. Only the gaps are work.

Our open gaps — this is the work

Since 2026-09-27 no open gap remains.

J21 (decided) — No rotating — it is stated how to rotate

The orientation is read and reported, but the image is not rotated. The reason is this module's scope: punkte() delivers the pixels as they stand in the file. A reader that silently rotates turns information into processing — and whoever measures rather than displays would afterwards hold coordinates that no longer match the file. Whoever displays rotates for themselves; so that nobody overlooks it, Bilder reports any orientation above 1 as a finding with its plain meaning. Pillow does not rotate by itself either — there it takes ImageOps.exif_transpose, an explicit step as well.

Deliberate decisions — nothing to do

J13 (decided) — Triangular upsampling, as libjpeg does

The specification leaves open how a subsampled chroma component returns to full size. The chosen method is libjpeg's — not because it is the prettier one but because it is the most widespread: whoever else looks at this image sees it this way. Only then can one check against a foreign decoder at all, and only then does "agrees" mean anything. The method applies only at a ratio of exactly two; at others libjpeg too takes the nearest neighbour.

J11 (decided) — Floating-point inverse transform, no integer method

libjpeg knows three inverse transforms — a slow accurate one, a fast integer one and a floating-point one. Here stands the cosine formula computed straightforwardly with a precomputed table. It is the slowest and the most accurate, and speed is not the question for this component: what is read are measurement files, not image streams. An integer method would moreover be a second source of deviation, to be held against libjpeg's third.

J19 (decided) — Read-only — and every boundary named

Nothing is written, for the same reason as with the compression methods: an encoder is twice the work and nobody who wants to open a foreign file needs it. The second half matters more: what is not read is named. A reader that half-reads a progressive file delivers an image — and an image looks like an image. Therefore every frame marker that is not the baseline method carries its own message with the method's name in it, and the test checks exactly that. Pillow in these cases reports either nothing or a Python exception with no statement about the file — which is right for an imaging library and not enough for file inspection.

Properties of the format

J16 (property) — Hierarchical JPEG (DHP, EXP, SOF5–7, SOF13–14) — closed on 2026-09-27

Several resolution levels on top of each other: a base image at low resolution, then differential frames whose reference is expanded after EXP to double width or height (mean rounded down, edge repeated, T.81 J.1.1.2). Differential frames have no DC prediction and no level shift; lossless differences are taken modulo 2^16. Test files: the ITU's T.83 data cost money; they were written by the JPEG reference software of ITU-T T.873 (thorfdbg/libjpeg 1.72), whose ITU licence explicitly permits use for conformance testing — ten files covering all six frame types. Measured: SOF5, SOF6, SOF13 and SOF14 bit-identical among themselves; against the reference software at most 5 over three levels, mean 1.1, lossless at most 3 (colour) or 1 (grey). The distance has two measured causes: T.81 does not specify the inverse transform bit-exactly, and the reference software carries the reference between levels at more than integer precision — rounding its base image down leaves exactly the transform differences in the lossless grey image. SOF15 is rejected by name because lossless arithmetic coding (SOF11) is missing.

J17 (property) — Arithmetically coded JPEG — closed on 2026-09-25

Own decoder Jpegarith following T.81 annexes D and F: the range coder with state table D.2, 64 bins per DC table and 256 per AC table, conditioning thresholds from DAC. SOF9 (sequential) and SOF10 (progressive, all four pass kinds) are read; SOF11 (lossless arithmetic) is rejected by name because no writer is available. Measured without tolerance: the same coefficients coded once with Huffman, once arithmetically (jpegtran -arithmetic, libjpeg-turbo 3.1.3) give bit-identical images — sequential, progressive, with a restart after every block. The counter-test sets a different K in DAC and gets a different image: the threshold is really read.

J15 (property) — Lossless JPEG (SOF3) — closed on 2026-09-25

Prediction from the neighbours (Ss 1 to 7), point transform (Al), 2 to 16 bits, with the edge rules of T.81 H.1.2.1: the first value of each scan and restart interval from 2^(P-Pt-1), the first row from Ra, the first value of every further row from Rb; the sum modulo 2^16, the difference 32768 as size 16 without extra bits. Bit-identical to the source at 8, 12 and 16 bits, predictors 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7, Pt 2, red-green-blue and restart every three rows (cjpeg -lossless, libjpeg-turbo 3.1.3). Sampling factors above 1 are rejected by name — libjpeg-turbo does not write them. Pillow reads 8 bits; 12 and 16 bits it does not recognise.

J3 (property) — Twelve bits per sample — closed on 2026-09-25

The planes are 16 bits wide since 2026-09-25; inverse transform, level shift (2048) and colour computation follow the depth. JpegDatei.werte() returns the full values, punkte() remains the 8-bit view (the upper bits). Measured against djpeg from libjpeg-turbo 3.1.3 for greyscale, 4:2:0 and progressive: at most two of 4095 counts apart — the rounding noise of the inverse transform, as at 8 bits. The 16-bit quantisation tables this reader already read now occur in an accepted file for the first time. Pillow does not recognise 12-bit JPEG.

J18 (property) — Four components — CMYK and YCCK — closed on 2026-09-25

Which colours four components carry is said only by the transform in Adobe's APP14: 2 is YCCK (the first three are YCbCr of an inverted CMY), 0 or no segment is CMYK as it stands; with the segment the values are also stored inverted. werte() returns C, M, Y, K as Pillow does, punkte() red-green-blue by Pillow's formula ((255 − K) · (1 − C/255) with its rounding). Measured on CMYK 4:4:4, YCCK 4:4:4 and YCCK 4:2:0 from TurboJPEG 3.1.3: at most two counts off Pillow. The counter-test sets the transform of a YCCK file to 0 and is then 255 off. Two components stay rejected by name — there is no agreed interpretation for them.

J7 (property) — The line count supplied afterwards, DNL — closed on 2026-09-25

If the frame header gives height zero, the reader looks for the DNL segment beforehand — behind the data of the first scan, across stuffed zeros and restart markers (T.81 B.2.5 permits it only there). After that everything runs as usual. No writer produces DNL, so the test file is built by hand: a libjpeg-turbo file with height zero and the segment supplied afterwards. It gives a bit-identical image to the original file. libjpeg-turbo 3.1.3 rejects it ("DNL not supported"), Pillow does not recognise it as JPEG. Without the segment the missing height is named.

J14 (property) — Progressive JPEG (SOF2) — closed on 2026-09-23

Until 2026-09-23 the largest single gap. Progressive is not an addendum to the baseline method: the coefficients arrive in several passes, split by frequency band and bit plane, and are therefore collected across all scans and transformed back only at the end. Two places where a reimplementation goes silently wrong: in a bit-plane refinement every coefficient that is already non-zero receives a correction bit, also inside the skipped run of zeros and in blocks of an end-of-block run; and a scan with a single component runs over that component’s own blocks, not over the MCUs — with 4:2:0 and a width that is not a multiple of 16 there are fewer. Measured in Progressivprobe: the same image written by the JDK JPEG encoder once sequentially and once progressively yields the same pixels, point for point, in colour 4:2:0, grey and with restart markers.

J6 (property) — Exif, ICC and thumbnails — closed on 2026-09-07

APP0 (JFIF), APP1 (Exif) and APP2 (ICC) are now read instead of skipped. The result comes out as a Jpegbeiwerk: pixel density together with its unit, orientation in plain words, a selection of twenty-two Exif tags, the colour profile's name, and the embedded thumbnail as bytes.

Exif is a TIFF file inside a JPEG file. After "Exif\0\0" comes a complete TIFF header, and it holds three traps, each with its own check. First the byte order: MM means big-endian, and skipping it reads orientation 6 as 1536. Second, every offset counts from the TIFF header, not from the start of the file — six bytes' difference, and every text lands displaced. Third, the thumbnail lives in the second directory (IFD1); stop after IFD0 and you never find it, and never notice, because everything else is right. Two counter-tests hit exactly these places and turn ten respectively one check red.

Why not this toolkit's TIFF reader. TiffDatei reads an image: it follows the strips, decompresses them and converts colours. Here nothing is to be decompressed, here two directories are to be read. Harnessing the image reader for that would mean checking every future change to it against a task it does not have. The shared structure is a dozen lines — the coupling would cost more than the repetition.

A broken sidecar is not a broken image. The pixels hang on none of these entries. An interpreter that aborts therefore does not discard the file: the segment is skipped via its length in every case — the read position stays with the main reader, not with the interpreter — and the failure is recorded as a finding. Losing a readable image over a side entry means confusing the scope.

The anchors. Three files from Pillow 12.3.0 with piexif 1.1.3, produced on 2026-09-07: one with a full sidecar (big-endian Exif, orientation 6, an 8-by-6 thumbnail), one with Exif but no thumbnail and orientation 1, and one with an sRGB profile and no Exif. The second and third are there because two distinctions could otherwise not be measured: no thumbnail is not the same as an empty one, and orientation 1 ("do not rotate") is not the same as no orientation at all.

J1 (property) — Two signature bytes are too few

FF D8 alone hits by accident: it is two bytes, and every other file with high byte values begins that way. What is checked is therefore FF D8 FF — a further marker must follow the start marker immediately, because in JPEG nothing but a segment ever stands between two markers. The counter-test for this is one of the twenty.

J8 (property) — Byte stuffing

See section 3. It is the only place where a single overlooked byte turns everything that follows into noise — without a message, because badly decoded Huffman codes are still valid Huffman codes.

J9 (property) — The DC term is a difference

Per component the zeroth coefficient is stored as a difference to the previous block of the same component. Read it as absolute and the image's brightness drifts away from left to right — visible, but not recognisable as an error, rather as a gradient. Restart markers reset this chain, and that is exactly why they exist.

J10 (property) — The zigzag applies twice

See section 3. The quantisation table is the part one forgets — and the error looks like compression. Two of the twenty counter-tests sit here, one per place of application.

J4 (property) — Restart markers are two things at once

An RSTn resets the DC chain and aligns the bit stream to the next byte boundary. Do only one of the two and the error starts only after the first restart interval — that is, never on a small image and immediately on a large one. A dedicated anchor with a restart every two blocks stands ready for this, and both halves have their own counter-test.

J2 (property) — Several tables in one segment

DQT and DHT may both carry several tables in a row, and whether an encoder does so is its own business: libjpeg writes four separate DHT segments, others merge them. Whoever stops after the first table later works with a table full of zeros and gets a grey image — never with one writer's files, always with the other's. A dedicated anchor stands ready whose four Huffman tables sit in one segment; it must yield pixel for pixel the same as the same content in four segments. Measured: Pillow reads both arrangements alike.

J12 (property) — The sampling plane rounds to whole MCUs

A component's plane is rounded up to whole MCUs, not to whole blocks — what sticks out beyond was written by the encoder only as padding and must not be read when upsampling at the edge, or the smoothing pulls padding into the image. That was visible on a 200×150 file with 21 counts of deviation, while everything else sat at 2. That the rounding itself is not measurable from outside is set out in 2.2 — it is a promise to the format, not a measured property.

Findings against the reference implementation

J5 (open there) — The restart interval does not come out

Measured: write a file with restart markers and read it back, and Pillow carries the interval nowhere — neither in info nor elsewhere. For displaying an image it is irrelevant; for the question of how a file is built it is the piece of information by which one recognises a writer. We expose it. That is not a bug in Pillow — it is an imaging library, not a file-inspection tool.

J20 (open there) — Four conditions without a statement about the file

Height zero with a supplied line count, two components, a truncated stream, a wrong signature — for all of these Pillow says either cannot identify image file or Truncated File Read, that is, nothing about what is wrong with the file. We name the place and the reason in every one of these cases. Again not a bug but a different purpose: it is the difference between displaying an image and being able to say what is in the file. No bug in Pillow was found in this measurement.

The Java implementation — where this reader stands among its own kind

Why against javax.imageio in particular. Every other comparison in this module measures against a tool in a different language — Pillow, astropy, pyarrow, meshio. That is good against misunderstandings of the format and says nothing about where this reader stands among its own kind. javax.imageio sits on every machine this module runs on and costs no foreign library — the module's rule stays untouched. It is therefore measured on every run and not once: Bildvergleichsprobe, with JPEG now 80 checks.

And for JPEG of all things the JDK reader is not a Java implementation. For PNG, BMP, GIF and TIFF it is pure Java; for JPEG it is not. Measured on the machine, not read up: com.sun.imageio.plugins.jpeg.JPEGImageReader has eleven native methods, com.sun.imageio.plugins.png.PNGImageReader not a single one, and the JDK's lib directory contains libjavajpeg.so carrying the string 6b 27-Mar-1998 — the IJG library of 1998. The JDK's JPEG reader is therefore a JNI wrapper around exactly the ancestor that Pillow reaches through libjpeg-turbo.

What the measurement is then still worth — and what it is not. It is not a comparison among its own kind but a second route to the same reference: Pillow speaks libjpeg-turbo, ImageIO speaks libjpeg 6b, two builds of the same core twenty-eight years apart. That this reader matches both within rounding distance is worth more than agreement with a single build — but it is not what section 7 means in the other documents, and is therefore expressly not called that here.

The pure Java reimplementation does exist, and until 2026-09-23 it drew the same line we did. Apache Commons Imaging carries a decoder without native code in org.apache.commons.imaging.formats.jpeg.decoder.JpegDecoder — and rejects everything that is not sequential baseline, with the message "Only sequential, baseline JPEGs are supported at the moment". Until 2026-09-23 that was exactly our line; since J14 this reader reads progressive files and so goes beyond the pure Java reimplementation.

Measured against Commons Imaging 1.0.0-alpha6 on 2026-09-06 — and this is the real measurement of this section, because here two reimplementations face each other and not two routes to libjpeg. Eleven anchors through both readers. At full chroma sampling (4:4:4) the distance is one, at the greyscale anchor zero — both read the same thing. At half chroma sampling it is around sixty; that is permitted, because the reconstruction of subsampled chroma is not specified at all in T.81, and demanding equality here measures the wrong thing (see section 1).

A finding against Commons Imaging, and of the kind that only measuring reveals. Anchor j6 carries a restart marker every two blocks. There Commons Imaging is off by up to 224 counts, on average by 50 — and growing with the distance from the start of the image: 107 in the first block row, 224 in the fifth. That is exactly what it looks like when the DC chain is not reset at the restart marker: the error accumulates. At the same nine support points that libjpeg supplied through Pillow we agree to within one count; Commons Imaging deviates widely at six of nine. That is not a difference of interpretation but a defect — and it is invisible as long as one reads small images without restart markers. That point J4 has two counter-tests here is precisely why that is not excess.

This measurement lies outside the module, in javavergleich/, because envisionz-framework-fileformats has the rule "no third-party libraries" — and it still holds. To read a single JPEG file Commons Imaging additionally needs commons-io and commons-lang3 on the class path; without them even Imaging.getBufferedImage fails with NoClassDefFoundError. That is the same operational note as for jHDF in the HDF5 document and not a criticism of the reimplementation.

Eleven anchors, and here for the first time no pixel comparison. For PNG, BMP, GIF and TIFF the test demands pixel equality, because those formats are lossless. JPEG is not: the inverse transform is specified only up to an accuracy bound — T.83 tests exactly that — and two correct implementations may differ by a few counts. What is measured is therefore the largest distance per channel, and across all eleven anchors it is three; on average it lies between 0.03 and 0.21 counts. Colour in 4:4:4, 4:2:2 and 4:2:0, quality 10 to 100, odd dimensions, restart markers, an image spanning several MCU rows, all four Huffman tables in one segment — nowhere more than three.

And the sRGB trap a third time, on a third reader of ours. The greyscale anchor j4 reported 74 counts of deviation through BufferedImage.getRGB() and one through the raster. It is the same trap as in PNG, BMP, GIF and TIFF: getRGB() returns not the pixel from the file but its conversion to sRGB, and ImageIO stores greyscale in a linear grey space. That it makes three independent readers of ours look alike is the best evidence that it does not sit with us. Both numbers stand as checks in the test — the 74 is the evidence, not the 1.

Whether the bound of three is a loophole — checked. A tolerance that lets everything through tests nothing. Two counter-tests: store the quantisation table row-wise instead of in zigzag and the distance rises to as much as 220; read the DC term as absolute instead of as a difference to the previous block and it rises to as much as 164. Both times the test goes red, and at every anchor. A distance of three is therefore tight enough to tell a format error from rounding noise.
