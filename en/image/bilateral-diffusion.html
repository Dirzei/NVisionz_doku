Edge-preserving denoising – bilateral filter and Perona-Malik

The computation behind Bilateral and PeronaMalik: why an ordinary mean smears edges, how a second weighting by brightness difference prevents this, how heat conduction that is inhibited at edges achieves the same step by step, pseudocode following the source, worked example, testing and limits.

What it is about

An image from an old video tape or a scan carries noise: every pixel deviates randomly by a few levels from its true value. The obvious counter-measure is averaging over the neighbourhood. For mm independent values the spread of the mean drops by the factor m\sqrt{m} – over a 7×7 window, to one seventh.

The ordinary mean has a price, though: it also averages across edges. If a pixel lies just beside the boundary of a bright object, the bright neighbours enter its mean; a sharp jump becomes a ramp as wide as the window. Yet that is exactly where the information that matters sits – outlines, lettering, the lines of a chart.

Both methods on this page solve this with the same basic idea: exchange only between points that look alike. A difference of a few levels is probably noise and is evened out; a difference of a hundred levels is probably an edge and is left alone. The bilateral filter implements this in a single pass as a weighting, Perona-Malik as a sequence of small exchange steps.

The third method of this section, total-variation denoising, is based on an optimisation problem and has its own page: Total variation after Chambolle.

The bilateral filter

Definition

For a pixel pp with value fpf_p a weighted mean is formed over the square window Nr(p)N_r(p) of (2r+1)2(2r+1)^2 points. Each neighbour qq receives two weights: one by position, one by value.

up=∑q∈Nr(p)ws(q−p)wr(fq−fp)fq∑q∈Nr(p)ws(q−p)wr(fq−fp)u_p = \frac{\sum_{q \in N_r(p)} w_s(q-p)\, w_r(f_q - f_p)\, f_q}{\sum_{q \in N_r(p)} w_s(q-p)\, w_r(f_q - f_p)}

ws(Δx,Δz)=exp⁡(−Δx2+Δz22σs2),wr(d)=exp⁡(−d22σr2)w_s(\Delta x, \Delta z) = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta x^2 + \Delta z^2}{2\sigma_s^2}\right), \qquad w_r(d) = \exp\left(-\frac{d^2}{2\sigma_r^2}\right)

Here σs\sigma_s is the width of the spatial weighting in pixels (sigmaOrt) and σr\sigma_r the width of the brightness weighting in grey levels (sigmaWert). If wrw_r were one everywhere, an ordinary Gaussian mean would remain. The range weighting makes the filter non-linear: the weight of a neighbour depends on the image itself.

How strongly the range weighting separates is shown by a small table with the defaults σr=12\sigma_r = 12 and σs=3\sigma_s = 3:

A neighbour on the other side of an edge of 60 levels thus counts roughly two hundred thousand times less than one on the same side – it practically drops out. Conversely, noise with a spread of 5 levels is evened out almost as by an ordinary mean.

Convention in the framework: The window is a square of radius rr, not a circle; the spatial weighting is not truncated, the window limits it. With the defaults r=3r = 3, σs=3\sigma_s = 3 the window corner still has the weight 0.37 – here the radius determines the reach more than σs\sigma_s. At the image border the edge value is continued (index clamped to [0,w−1][0, w-1] or [0,h−1][0, h-1]). The constructor enforces r≥1r \ge 1, σs≥0.1\sigma_s \ge 0.1 and σr≥0.1\sigma_r \ge 0.1.

Pseudocode – Bilateral.anwenden(e, w, h)

// spatial weights once in advance, for the whole window
for dz ← −r to r, dx ← −r to r:
    pos[dz, dx] ← exp(−(dx² + dz²) / (2·σs²))
denominator ← 2·σr²

for each pixel (x, z):
    centre ← e[z, x];  sum ← 0;  weight ← 0
    for dz ← −r to r:
        zz ← clamp(z + dz, 0, h−1)             continue the edge value
        for dx ← −r to r:
            xx ← clamp(x + dx, 0, w−1)
            v ← e[zz, xx];  d ← v − centre
            g ← pos[dz, dx] · exp(−d² / denominator)
            sum ← sum + g·v;  weight ← weight + g
    out[z, x] ← if weight > 0 then sum/weight else centre
return out                                     input stays unchanged

Source: envisionz-framework-image/src/envisionz/image/Bilateral.java. The point itself is always in the window with weight one; the denominator is therefore never zero and the fallback does not apply in practice.

Because all weights are positive, the result is a convex combination of the neighbour values: it always lies between the smallest and the largest value in the window. No new extreme values, overshoots or halos at edges arise.

Anisotropic diffusion after Perona and Malik

From heat conduction to inhibited heat conduction

If brightness is thought of as temperature, an ordinary Gaussian mean is nothing but heat conduction: after the time tt the heat equation

∂u∂t=∇⋅(∇u)=Δu\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nabla \cdot (\nabla u) = \Delta u

has convolved the initial image exactly with a Gaussian of width 2t\sqrt{2t}. Heat flows from warm to cold, proportional to the gradient, and equally well everywhere – across edges too.

Perona and Malik make the conductivity depend on the gradient itself:

∂u∂t=∇⋅(g(|∇u|)∇u),g(s)=11+s2/K2\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nabla \cdot \big(g(|\nabla u|)\, \nabla u\big), \qquad g(s) = \frac{1}{1 + s^2/K^2}

For a small gradient g≈1g \approx 1 and the equation behaves like heat conduction. For a gradient far above the threshold KK, gg goes to zero – hardly anything flows across an edge. What matters is the flux Φ(s)=g(s)s\Phi(s) = g(s)\,s:

Φ(d)=d1+d2/K2,Φ'(d)=1−d2/K2(1+d2/K2)2\Phi(d) = \frac{d}{1 + d^2/K^2}, \qquad \Phi'(d) = \frac{1 - d^2/K^2}{(1 + d^2/K^2)^2}

The flux has its maximum K/2K/2 at d=Kd = K and decreases beyond. This has a remarkable consequence: for differences below KK the method smooths; above it, a steeper step exchanges less than a flat one – an edge is not only spared, it can even sharpen slightly. The threshold KK is thus the boundary between "still noise" and "already an edge" and belongs in the order of magnitude of the noise, not of the edges.

The discretisation

The equation is implemented as by Perona and Malik themselves: explicit in time, with the differences to the four direct neighbours instead of a gradient magnitude. With dj=uj−uid_j = u_j - u_i for the neighbours left, right, above and below, one step reads

ui(n+1)=ui(n)+λ∑j=14dj1+dj2/K2u_i^{(n+1)} = u_i^{(n)} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{4} \frac{d_j}{1 + d_j^2/K^2}

Two properties follow directly:

  • The sum is conserved. The flux is odd, Φ(−d)=−Φ(d)\Phi(-d) = -\Phi(d): what one neighbour gives, the other receives. At the border the access points, through the edge continuation, to the point itself; there d=0d = 0 and nothing flows out. Brightness is redistributed, but neither created nor destroyed.
  • No new extreme values as long as λ≤1/4\lambda \le 1/4. Writing the step as ui+λ∑jgjdj=(1−λ∑jgj)ui+λ∑jgjuju_i + \lambda \sum_j g_j d_j = (1 - \lambda\sum_j g_j)\,u_i + \lambda \sum_j g_j u_j with 0<gj≤10 < g_j \le 1, for λ≤1/4\lambda \le 1/4 all coefficients are non-negative and sum to one. The new uiu_i is again a convex combination – the discrete maximum principle. With larger λ\lambda the exchange overshoots and builds up.
Convention in the framework: Default new PeronaMalik() = 10 steps, threshold K=12K = 12, step size λ=0.20\lambda = 0.20. The constructor limits λ\lambda to the interval [0.01,0.25][0.01, 0.25], the number of steps to at least 1 and KK to at least 0.1. The threshold is a fixed number in grey levels, not estimated from the image; it refers to the value range of the material (usually 16 to 235 for luminance).

Pseudocode – PeronaMalik.anwenden(e, w, h)

a ← copy of e;  k2 ← K²
repeat steps times:
    for each pixel i = (x, z):
        centre ← a[i];  sum ← 0
        for the four neighbours (x−1,z), (x+1,z), (x,z−1), (x,z+1):
            (xx, zz) ← clamped to the image       at the border: the point itself, d = 0
            d ← a[zz, xx] − centre
            sum ← sum + d / (1 + d²/k2)            conductivity falls with d²
        b[i] ← centre + λ · sum
    a ← b                                        all points simultaneously: explicit
return a

Source: envisionz-framework-image/src/envisionz/image/PeronaMalik.java. Each step reads only the result of the previous one; the order in which points are visited therefore does not matter.

Worked example

The test image has 128 × 128 pixels: a horizontal ramp 60+0.5x60 + 0.5\,x as background, with a circle of radius 35 and value 180 in it. Normally distributed noise with standard deviation 14 is added (fixed random seed). The distance to the noise-free image is measured as peak signal-to-noise ratio

PSNR⁡=10log10⁡25521N∑i(ui−fi)2dB\mathrm{PSNR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{255^2}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i (u_i - f_i)^2}\ \text{dB}

Two things can be read from this. First: with σr=12\sigma_r = 12, the bilateral filter falls well short of its potential for noise with a spread of 14. The difference between two noisy points of the same area has the standard deviation 2⋅14≈20\sqrt{2}\cdot 14 \approx 20 levels; many neighbours therefore receive only a small range weight, and outliers stand almost alone. Only with σr\sigma_r above the noise does it work fully. Second: Perona-Malik first improves with the number of steps and then gets worse again – after 50 steps the flatter parts of the image are attacked as well.

On a small scale it is shown by a single row of eight values with a jump of about a hundred levels, (50,54,47,52,150,146,153,149)(50, 54, 47, 52, 150, 146, 153, 149), computed as an image of height one:

The bilateral filter smooths each side on its own; the weights across the jump (d≥96d \ge 96) are below 10−1310^{-13}. Perona-Malik exchanges a small but non-vanishing flux across the jump (Φ(100)≈1.4\Phi(100) \approx 1.4). After 100 steps the jump has shrunk from 98 to 75 levels – the edge is spared, but not frozen. With Perona-Malik the sum of the eight values is the same in both rows as at the start, 801 (to the rounding of the table); the bilateral filter does not conserve it.

How it is tested

The probe Filterprobe generates its own test images and uses only target values that follow from the mathematics:

  • Bilateral, fixed point: a constant image stays constant (deviation at most 10−910^{-9}).
  • Bilateral, bound: the result lies within the value range of the input – a consequence of the convex combination.
  • Bilateral, edge: a step of height 1000 at σr=1\sigma_r = 1 stays exactly: the weight of the other side is e−500000e^{-500000}, exactly zero in double.
  • Bilateral, linear reproduction: with σr=109\sigma_r = 10^9 a symmetric Gaussian mean remains; it reproduces a linear ramp 3x+2z3x + 2z in the interior (deviation below 10−610^{-6}).
  • Perona-Malik, fixed point: constant image unchanged after 25 steps at λ=0.25\lambda = 0.25, without any tolerance.
  • Perona-Malik, conservation: the sum over the image is conserved after 20 steps (relative 10−810^{-8}).
  • Perona-Malik, maximum principle: no new extreme values – not even when λ=5\lambda = 5 is requested; the constructor must limit it to 0.25.
  • Perona-Malik, flux bound: since |Φ|≤K/2|\Phi| \le K/2, a point changes in nn steps by at most 4λ⋅K/2⋅n=2nλK4\lambda \cdot K/2 \cdot n = 2 n \lambda K; tested on a step of height 500.
  • For both: input unchanged, result length, 1×1 image, images narrower than the kernel, shift equivariance (a constant added to the image shifts the result by the same constant, deviation below 10−810^{-8}) and extreme values ±106\pm 10^6 within the value range.

Limits

  • Single outliers largely survive the bilateral filter: a point that differs from all neighbours by far more than σr\sigma_r receives hardly any weight from them and stays with itself. For impulse noise a median belongs before the denoising.
  • Cost: the bilateral filter computes (2r+1)2(2r+1)^2 exponential functions per pixel, 49 at r=3r = 3; there is no fast approximation (grid, decomposition). Large radii become correspondingly expensive.
  • One fixed threshold for the whole image: neither σr\sigma_r nor KK adapts. In dark and bright regions with noise of different strength, one number does not suit both.
  • Perona-Malik does not stop by itself. The flux across an edge is small, but not zero; with many steps edges shrink too. The number of steps is the real control variable. Many steps also produce a "painted" look: areas become completely smooth, fine texture disappears.
  • Staircasing: in gentle ramps whose gradient lies near KK, the backward diffusion beyond KK tends to form steps.
  • Direction dependence: Perona-Malik computes with the four axis-parallel neighbours; diagonal edges are treated somewhat differently from horizontal and vertical ones.
  • Value range: both methods work on a plane in double. Thresholds in levels assume that the plane is in the value range of the material, not normalised to 0 to 1.
