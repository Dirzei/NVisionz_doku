Removing defects – the notch filter

The computation behind Kerbfilter: what a periodic interference pattern looks like in the spectrum, why it can be separated cleanly from the image there, how the peaks are found and notched, pseudocode following the source, worked example, testing and limits.

What it is about

Some defects are not random but regular: the chroma crosstalk of a VHS tape lying over the picture as a fine oblique stripe pattern; moiré when scanning a printed halftone image; the grid of graph paper showing through from the back. In the image itself such patterns are hard to grasp – they cover everything and are equally strong everywhere. A median or a denoiser only removes them if it destroys the fine detail of the image at the same time.

In the frequency domain the situation is quite different. An oscillation with a fixed period and direction occupies exactly two positions there. The image itself spreads its energy widely, concentrated at the low frequencies. An interference pattern therefore sticks out as a narrow, tall peak from a broad background. Setting these peaks to zero and transforming back removes the pattern, and the image loses only the few frequencies the pattern occupied.

That is why the notch filter counts as a reconstruction in the module (art() = REKONSTRUKTION), not as an estimate: the pattern is superimposed on the image, not interwoven with it; removing it adds nothing guessed.

A periodic pattern in the spectrum

The two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform of a square plane of side length nn is

Fl,k=∑z=0n−1∑x=0n−1fz,xe−2πi(kx+lz)/nF_{l,k} = \sum_{z=0}^{n-1} \sum_{x=0}^{n-1} f_{z,x}\, e^{-2\pi i (k x + l z)/n}

with the column frequency kk and the row frequency ll, both from 00 to n−1n-1. The frequency kk and the frequency n−kn-k are the same oscillation with opposite sign; the highest frequencies therefore lie in the middle of the field, the lowest in the four corners. How the fast transform works is described by framework-math under Fourier and cosine transforms.

A pattern fz,x=Acos⁡(2πk0x/n)f_{z,x} = A \cos(2\pi k_0 x / n) with an integer number of periods k0k_0 gives

F0,k0=F0,n−k0=An22,otherwise 0F_{0,k_0} = F_{0,n-k_0} = \frac{A\, n^2}{2}, \qquad \text{otherwise } 0

Two peaks, mirror images through the origin, each with half the amplitude times the number of pixels. This holds generally: for a real image F−l,−k=Fl,kF_{-l,-k} = \overline{F_{l,k}} – the spectrum is point-symmetric. Whoever removes a peak must also remove its mirror image, otherwise the result of the inverse transform is no longer real.

The method as implemented

Preparation

The fast transform in framework-math requires a power of two as side length. The plane is therefore padded to a square of side length n=2⌈log2⁡max⁡(w,h)⌉n = 2^{\lceil \log_2 \max(w,h) \rceil}, and with the edge value, not with zeros: a hard edge towards a black border would produce a cross of peaks along the axes in the spectrum that looks like an interference pattern. The mean is subtracted from the padded field, then the forward transform is computed.

Searching and testing peaks

From real and imaginary parts the magnitude |F||F| of each position is formed, and once the median MM of all magnitudes. Then, at most hoechstzahl times:

  1. Find the largest position outside the centre. A disc of radius R0R_0 (mittenradius) around frequency zero is excluded. Because frequency zero lies in the corners, the distance is measured cyclically: Δx=min⁡(x,n−x)\Delta x = \min(x, n-x), Δz=min⁡(z,n−z)\Delta z = \min(z, n-z); excluded is Δx2+Δz2≤R02\Delta x^2 + \Delta z^2 \le R_0^2. Basic brightness and coarse image structure sit there; notching them would destroy the image.
  2. Test against the local surroundings. Mean UU of the magnitudes in a ring around the peak, from radius rk+1r_k + 1 to rk+5r_k + 5 (with the notch radius rkr_k). The peak counts only if |F|≥s⋅U|F| \ge s \cdot U with the threshold ss (schwelle).
  3. Test against the whole spectrum. In addition |F|≥2M|F| \ge 2M must hold.
  4. Notch. All positions in a disc of radius rkr_k around the peak and around its mirror image ((n−z)modn,(n−x)modn)((n-z) \bmod n,\ (n-x) \bmod n) are set to zero – real part, imaginary part and magnitude, so that the next search does not find them again.

If a peak fails one of the two tests, the search ends; the further candidates are smaller. Then the inverse transform is computed, the mean added back and the original section w×hw \times h cut out.

Why against the surroundings and not against the median

A natural image does have strong components outside the centre – at edges, in textures – but they are broad: an edge produces a line in the spectrum, a texture a halo. An interference pattern is narrow, a few positions high above their surroundings. Measured against the median of the whole spectrum, according to the source comment, the filter also triggered on completely clean material and made it worse. The ring around the peak asks the right question: does this position stand out above its immediate neighbourhood? The median test remains as a second, weaker safeguard.

Convention in the framework: Default new Kerbfilter() = centre radius 8, notch radius 3, threshold 6.0, at most 24 peaks. The constructor sets centre and notch radius to at least 1, the threshold to at least 1.5 and the maximum count to at least 1. A notched peak counts once, even though its mirror image is notched with it; gekerbteSpitzen() returns this number.
Convention in the framework: The notch is a hard disc: zero inside, unchanged outside, without a soft transition. The inverse transform divides by n2n^2 (Fft2d.fft2 with rueckwaerts = true). The mean is subtracted beforehand and added back afterwards; it lies in the excluded centre region anyway.

Pseudocode – Kerbfilter.anwenden(e, w, h)

n ← next power of two ≥ max(w, h)
re ← pad(e, w, h, n)                         with the edge value
im ← 0
mean ← mean(re);  re ← re − mean
fft2(re, im, n, forward)
mag[i] ← √(re[i]² + im[i]²);  M ← median(mag)
notched ← 0
repeat at most maxCount times:
    best ← position with largest mag for which
           min(x, n−x)² + min(z, n−z)² > R0²        cyclic distance to the origin
    if none found: stop
    U ← mean of mag in the ring r_k+1 ≤ distance ≤ r_k+5 around best   cyclic
    if mag[best] < s·U: stop
    if mag[best] < 2·M: stop
    notch(best);  notch(mirror image of best)     disc of radius r_k: re, im, mag ← 0
    notched ← notched + 1
fft2(re, im, n, inverse)                      divides by n²
re ← re + mean
return section w × h of re

Source: envisionz-framework-image/src/envisionz/image/Kerbfilter.java, with envisionz.math.Fft2d (naechstePotenz, auffuellen, fft2, ausschneiden) from framework-math. Cost: two transforms of size n2log2⁡nn^2 \log_2 n and, per peak, one pass over all n2n^2 magnitudes.

Worked example

The peaks by hand

The image from the probe: 64 × 64 points, basic brightness 128, a slow oscillation with one period across the image width and amplitude 20, and on top of it a disturbance with 16 periods and amplitude 15:

fz,x=128+20cos⁡2πx64+15cos⁡2π⋅16x64f_{z,x} = 128 + 20 \cos\frac{2\pi x}{64} + 15 \cos\frac{2\pi \cdot 16\, x}{64}

After subtracting the mean 128, the spectrum has exactly four non-zero positions, all in row l=0l = 0:

The largest position outside radius 8 is (0, 16). Its ring is empty, UU is practically zero, and the median of all magnitudes is zero too – both tests are passed. Position (0, 16) and its mirror image (0, 48) are notched. The next search finds only zeros outside the centre and stops. Result: gekerbteSpitzen() = 1, the disturbance has vanished to within 10−610^{-6}, the useful oscillation still has amplitude 20, the basic brightness is 128.

An oblique pattern that does not fit the grid

Real patterns rarely have an integer number of periods. Test image 100 × 80: ramp 60+0.5x60 + 0.5\,x, circle of radius 25 and value 180, on top 12sin⁡(2π(0.23x+0.11z))12 \sin\big(2\pi(0.23\,x + 0.11\,z)\big). It is padded to 128 × 128; there the pattern would have 29.44 and 14.08 periods – not integers – and it fills only the image part of the padded field. Its energy is therefore spread over several neighbouring positions (leakage).

The filter removes most of the pattern, but not all of it: the smeared energy extends over more positions than two notches of radius 3 cover. On the undisturbed image it does not trigger. The module documentation gives, for its own test image with a periodic pattern, 26.04 dB before and 46.27 dB after.

How it is tested

Filterprobe works with images whose spectrum is known – side length 64, so that padding adds nothing and the frequencies lie on the grid:

  • Check of the test image: the projection onto the interfering oscillation yields exactly the amplitude 15 (to 10−910^{-9}).
  • One disturbance: exactly one peak notched; afterwards the amplitude of the disturbance is zero, the useful signal at frequency 1 is still 20, the basic brightness 128 – each to 10−610^{-6}.
  • Clean image: nothing notched, image unchanged (to 10−610^{-6}).
  • Constant image: after subtracting the mean the spectrum is empty; nothing notched, unchanged (to 10−910^{-9}).
  • Two disturbances (16 periods horizontally, 20 vertically): two notches, both amplitudes zero afterwards (to 10−610^{-6}).
  • Kind: REKONSTRUKTION. 1×1 image: unchanged, there is only frequency zero.
  • Together with all filters: input unchanged, shift equivariance (a constant on the image shifts the result by exactly that constant), tiny images.

Limits

  • Low frequencies are left alone. A pattern with at most R0=8R_0 = 8 periods across the padded side length lies in the centre region and is not found – slow brightness waves, for example.
  • Leakage. Patterns that do not fit the field an integer number of times smear over several positions. The notch with a fixed radius takes the core, the tails remain. There is no window before the transform.
  • Hard notch. The disc sets to zero without a transition. The image content that happened to lie on these frequencies is lost as well; with large notch radii and many notches this becomes visible.
  • Patterns that change across the image, such as a grid distorted towards the edge, have no narrow peak but a smeared spot and fail the ring test.
  • Padding costs. 720 × 576 becomes 1024 × 1024 – about 2.5 times the area. The memory requirement is a few fields of that size.
  • Harmonics: a non-sinusoidal pattern (square grid) has peaks at multiples of its fundamental frequency. Each counts as a peak of its own; the limit is 24 peaks.
