Curves, splines and surfaces – background

The computation behind Pchip, CubicSpline, Akima1D, Spline3D, BSpline, Nurbs and NurbsFlaeche: cubic Hermite pieces and the different routes to their slopes, the B-spline basis, knot insertion, global interpolation, rational curves and exact surfaces of revolution – with pseudocode following the source, recomputed examples, checks and limits.

What this is about

Two tasks come up constantly in everyday engineering. The first: intermediate values from a table – a material property λ(T)\lambda(T), a compressor characteristic, digitised points of a curve. The second: geometry – a blade contour through measured points, an annulus contour as a surface of revolution, a circular arc that is meant to be exactly a circular arc.

For both, the curve is assembled piecewise from polynomials that join smoothly at the breakpoints – splines. Which pieces and which joining conditions are chosen decides the properties:

CubicSplinecubic, natural splineC2C^2smoothest, overshoots at steps

Spline3Dcubic, Catmull-RomC1C^1point sequences in space, every point is hit
BSplineany degree, any dimensionCp−1C^{p-1} with simple knotsCAD curves, local control, interpolation
Nurbs, NurbsFlaecherationalas B-splineconic sections and surfaces of revolution exactly

Cubic Hermite pieces

The first four classes consist of cubic pieces that are fixed on each interval [xk,xk+1][x_k, x_{k+1}], h=xk+1−xkh = x_{k+1} - x_k, by values yk,yk+1y_k, y_{k+1} and slopes dk,dk+1d_k, d_{k+1} at the ends. With s=(t−xk)/hs = (t - x_k)/h:

p(t)=h00(s)yk+h10(s)hdk+h01(s)yk+1+h11(s)hdk+1,p(t) = h_{00}(s)\,y_k + h_{10}(s)\,h\,d_k + h_{01}(s)\,y_{k+1} + h_{11}(s)\,h\,d_{k+1},

h00=2s3−3s2+1,h10=s3−2s2+s,h01=−2s3+3s2,h11=s3−s2.h_{00} = 2s^3 - 3s^2 + 1,\quad h_{10} = s^3 - 2s^2 + s,\quad h_{01} = -2s^3 + 3s^2,\quad h_{11} = s^3 - s^2.

Values and slopes match at every breakpoint, so the curve is continuously differentiable (C1C^1). The whole difference between PCHIP, Akima and Catmull-Rom lies in how the slopes dkd_k are chosen; the natural cubic spline determines them indirectly via second derivatives.

PCHIP: shape-preserving

The method of Fritsch and Carlson (1980) with the slopes after Fritsch and Butland (1984) – the method behind pchip in MATLAB – chooses the slopes such that the curve takes on the shape of the data. With the secants δk=(yk+1−yk)/hk\delta_k = (y_{k+1} - y_k)/h_k:

dk=w1+w2w1/δk−1+w2/δk,w1=2hk+hk−1,w2=hk+2hk−1,d_k = \frac{w_1 + w_2}{w_1/\delta_{k-1} + w_2/\delta_k}, \qquad w_1 = 2h_k + h_{k-1},\quad w_2 = h_k + 2h_{k-1},

and dk=0d_k = 0 if one of the two secants is zero or they have different signs.

The weighted harmonic mean always lies between the two secants and is never larger than three times the smaller one – according to Fritsch and Carlson a sufficient condition for a cubic Hermite piece between two monotone points to stay monotone. At a local extremum of the data (sign change of the secants) the slope is 0, and the extremum of the curve lies there as well; plateaus stay flat.

At the ends a one-sided three-point formula with limiting applies (Moler, pchipend):

d0=(2h0+h1)δ0−h0δ1h0+h1,d_0 = \frac{(2h_0 + h_1)\,\delta_0 - h_0\,\delta_1}{h_0 + h_1},

set to 0 if its sign differs from that of δ0\delta_0, and limited to 3δ03\delta_0 if δ0\delta_0 and δ1\delta_1 have different signs and |d0|>|3δ0||d_0| > |3\delta_0|; mirrored at the right end. With two points the curve is the straight line.

Pseudocode – Pchip.steigungen(x, y)

h_k ← x_{k+1} − x_k;  δ_k ← (y_{k+1} − y_k)/h_k
if n = 2: d ← (δ_0, δ_0);  done
for k ← 1 to n−2:
    if δ_{k−1} = 0 or δ_k = 0 or sign(δ_{k−1}) ≠ sign(δ_k):  d_k ← 0
    else:  w1 ← 2h_k + h_{k−1};  w2 ← h_k + 2h_{k−1};  d_k ← (w1 + w2)/(w1/δ_{k−1} + w2/δ_k)
d_0 ← end(h_0, h_1, δ_0, δ_1);   d_{n−1} ← end(h_{n−2}, h_{n−3}, δ_{n−2}, δ_{n−3})

end(h1, h2, δ1, δ2):
    dd ← ((2h1 + h2) δ1 − h1 δ2)/(h1 + h2)
    if sign(dd) ≠ sign(δ1): dd ← 0
    else if sign(δ1) ≠ sign(δ2) and |dd| > |3δ1|: dd ← 3δ1
    return dd

Natural cubic spline

The cubic spline additionally requires continuous second derivatives. Written in terms of the second derivatives MiM_i at the nodes, continuity of the first derivative leads to a tridiagonal system

hi−1Mi−1+2(hi−1+hi)Mi+hiMi+1=6(yi+1−yihi−yi−yi−1hi−1),M0=Mn−1=0,h_{i-1} M_{i-1} + 2(h_{i-1} + h_i)\,M_i + h_i M_{i+1} = 6\Big(\frac{y_{i+1} - y_i}{h_i} - \frac{y_i - y_{i-1}}{h_{i-1}}\Big), \qquad M_0 = M_{n-1} = 0,

“natural” because of the vanishing curvature at the ends. It is solved with the Thomas algorithm (Gaussian elimination without pivoting for tridiagonal matrices, O(n)O(n)). Evaluation with a=(xk+1−t)/ha = (x_{k+1} - t)/h, b=(t−xk)/hb = (t - x_k)/h:

S(t)=ayk+byk+1+((a3−a)Mk+(b3−b)Mk+1)h26.S(t) = a\,y_k + b\,y_{k+1} + \big((a^3 - a) M_k + (b^3 - b) M_{k+1}\big)\frac{h^2}{6}.

Among all interpolating C2C^2 functions the spline minimises the bending energy ∫(S'')2\int (S'')^2 – it is the “stiffest” curve, but precisely for that reason it overshoots at steps: in the figure it reaches 1.080. Outside [x0,xn−1][x_0, x_{n-1}], eval returns the end value (constant).

Akima

Akima (1970) chooses the slope at xix_i as a weighted mean of the two adjacent secants mi−1,mim_{i-1}, m_i, weighted with the differences of the secants on the respective other side:

di=|mi+1−mi|mi−1+|mi−1−mi−2|mi|mi+1−mi|+|mi−1−mi−2|.d_i = \frac{|m_{i+1} - m_i|\,m_{i-1} + |m_{i-1} - m_{i-2}|\,m_i}{|m_{i+1} - m_i| + |m_{i-1} - m_{i-2}|}.

If both weights are zero, di=12(mi−1+mi)d_i = \tfrac12 (m_{i-1} + m_i). Where the data run straight on one side, the secant of that side gets the full weight – the curve stays calm at kinks. At the ends, as in the Fortran original AKIMA.F, two phantom secants each are extrapolated linearly (m−1=2m0−m1m_{-1} = 2m_0 - m_1 etc.). Outside, extrapolation is linear with the end slope. Monotonicity is not guaranteed (figure: 1.074).

Source: envisionz-framework-math/src/envisionz/math/kurven/Akima1D.java (from framework-data). Probe NM4-AK-06 compares with an exact rational computation after Akima to 10−1510^{-15}.

Catmull-Rom for point sequences in space

Spline3D refines a sequence of points PiP_i in space into a smooth line through every point – for 3D charts and operating lines. Each piece is a Hermite piece between PiP_i and Pi+1P_{i+1} with the tangents

mi=c(Pi+1−Pi−1),c=tension,m_i = c\,(P_{i+1} - P_{i-1}), \qquad c = \text{tension},

at the start and end with the end point itself as the missing neighbour. Per segment perSegment points at t=s/perSegmentt = s/\text{perSegment} are generated, finally the end point.

Convention in the framework: the tension is the factor cc in front of the difference. c=0.5c = 0.5 is the classic, uniformly parametrised Catmull-Rom spline; c=0c = 0 sets all tangents to zero (a corner at every point), larger values make the curve swing out more. The centripetal variant of the Catmull-Rom spline (knot spacing by the square root of the chord length) is not computed; until 2026-09-28 the class description wrongly called c=0.5c = 0.5 “centripetal” (corrected, WT5).

Example. Points (0,0,0)(0, 0, 0), (1,1,0)(1, 1, 0), (2,0,0)(2, 0, 0), two points per segment, c=0.5c = 0.5: in the first segment m0=0.5((1,1)−(0,0))=(0.5,0.5)m_0 = 0.5\,((1, 1) - (0, 0)) = (0.5,\ 0.5) and m1=0.5((2,0)−(0,0))=(1,0)m_1 = 0.5\,((2, 0) - (0, 0)) = (1, 0); at t=0.5t = 0.5 this gives (0.4375,0.5625,0)(0.4375,\ 0.5625,\ 0). Spline3D.evaluate returns exactly these values.

B-splines

A B-spline of degree pp is a sum of control points weighted with basis functions:

C(u)=∑i=0nNi,p(u)Pi.C(u) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} N_{i,p}(u)\, P_i.

The basis functions are defined recursively over a non-decreasing knot vector U=(u0,…,un+p+1)U = (u_0, \dots, u_{n+p+1}) (Cox and de Boor):

Ni,0(u)={1,ui≤u<ui+10,otherwiseNi,p(u)=u−uiui+p−uiNi,p−1(u)+ui+p+1−uui+p+1−ui+1Ni+1,p−1(u).N_{i,0}(u) = \begin{cases} 1, & u_i \le u < u_{i+1} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad N_{i,p}(u) = \frac{u - u_i}{u_{i+p} - u_i} N_{i,p-1}(u) + \frac{u_{i+p+1} - u}{u_{i+p+1} - u_{i+1}} N_{i+1,p-1}(u).

The properties CAD relies on: Ni,p≥0N_{i,p} \ge 0; ∑iNi,p(u)=1\sum_i N_{i,p}(u) = 1 (partition of unity), so the curve lies in the convex hull of the control points; Ni,pN_{i,p} is non-zero only on [ui,ui+p+1)[u_i, u_{i+p+1}) – a control point acts locally on at most p+1p + 1 knot spans. If the first and last knot are repeated p+1p + 1 times (clamped knot vector), the curve starts and ends at P0P_0 and PnP_n. With U=(0,…,0,1,…,1)U = (0, \dots, 0, 1, \dots, 1) the B-spline is a Bézier curve.

Evaluation after Piegl and Tiller

  • A2.1 – span: binary search for the ii with ui≤u<ui+1u_i \le u < u_{i+1}; at the right end the last non-empty span.
  • A2.2 – basis: the p+1p + 1 non-vanishing functions Ni−p,p…Ni,pN_{i-p,p} \dots N_{i,p} in the triangular scheme of the recursion, without division by zero for multiple knots.
  • A2.3, A3.2 – derivatives: the derivatives of the basis up to order k≤pk \le p from the same scheme; orders above pp are 0.
// BSpline.basis(i, u, p, U) – A2.2
N[0] ← 1
for j ← 1 to p:
    left[j] ← u − U[i+1−j];   right[j] ← U[i+j] − u
    saved ← 0
    for r ← 0 to j−1:
        t ← N[r] / (right[r+1] + left[j−r])
        N[r] ← saved + right[r+1] · t
        saved ← left[j−r] · t
    N[j] ← saved
// point (A3.1): C ← Σ_{j=0..p} N[j] · P[i−p+j];  u outside is clamped to the range

Quadratic Bézier curve with P0=(0,0)P_0 = (0, 0), P1=(1,2)P_1 = (1, 2), P2=(2,0)P_2 = (2, 0), U=(0,0,0,1,1,1)U = (0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1): at u=0.5u = 0.5 the basis values are (14,12,14)(\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14), hence C=(1,1)C = (1, 1); the first derivative is (2,0)(2, 0), the second (0,−8)(0, -8). ableitungen(0.5, 2) returns exactly that.

Knot insertion

An additional knot u\bar u in span kk does not change the curve but gives it one more control point (Böhm 1980, A5.1):

Qi=αiPi+(1−αi)Pi−1,αi=u−uiui+p−ui,k−p+1≤i≤k;Q_i = \alpha_i P_i + (1 - \alpha_i) P_{i-1}, \qquad \alpha_i = \frac{\bar u - u_i}{u_{i+p} - u_i}, \qquad k - p + 1 \le i \le k;

before that the PiP_i stay, after it they shift by one position. In the example above the knot 0.5 gives the control points (0,0),(0.5,1),(1.5,1),(2,0)(0, 0), (0.5,\ 1), (1.5,\ 1), (2, 0) – both α=0.5\alpha = 0.5 –, and every curve point stays the same to rounding. Knot insertion is the basis for subdivision, derivatives at knots and data exchange with CAD.

Global interpolation

BSpline.interpoliere(daten, grad) lays a curve exactly through mm points QkQ_k (Piegl and Tiller, A9.1):

  1. Parameters by chord length: u0=0\bar u_0 = 0, uk=uk−1+|Qk−Qk−1|/L\bar u_k = \bar u_{k-1} + |Q_k - Q_{k-1}|/L, um−1=1\bar u_{m-1} = 1 (or given).
  2. Knots by averaging (eq. 9.8): clamped, inside uj+p=1p∑i=jj+p−1uiu_{j+p} = \frac1p \sum_{i=j}^{j+p-1} \bar u_i – this keeps the system of equations well conditioned.
  3. System NP=QN P = Q with Nk,i=Ni,p(uk)N_{k,i} = N_{i,p}(\bar u_k), solved per coordinate with the singular value decomposition (GolubKahan); if the condition exceeds 101410^{14}, the interpolation is rejected (“parameters too close?”).

Five points (0,0),(1,1),(2,0),(3,1),(4,0)(0,0), (1,1), (2,0), (3,1), (4,0), cubic: all chords equally long, parameters (0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1)(0,\ 0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.75,\ 1), knots (0,0,0,0,0.5,1,1,1,1)(0, 0, 0, 0, 0.5, 1, 1, 1, 1) with the averaged interior knot (0.25+0.5+0.75)/3=0.5(0.25 + 0.5 + 0.75)/3 = 0.5. NURBS: rational curves

Polynomial splines cannot represent a circle exactly. NURBS (non-uniform rational B-splines) can, with weights wi>0w_i > 0:

C(u)=∑iNi,p(u)wiPi∑iNi,p(u)wi.C(u) = \frac{\sum_i N_{i,p}(u)\, w_i P_i}{\sum_i N_{i,p}(u)\, w_i}.

The computation uses homogeneous coordinates: the points (wiPi,wi)(w_i P_i,\ w_i) form an ordinary B-spline in Rd+1\mathbb{R}^{d+1}, and the curve point is its projection. The derivatives follow from the quotient rule in the form of Piegl and Tiller (A4.2): with A(u)=∑NwPA(u) = \sum N w P and w(u)=∑Nww(u) = \sum N w,

C(k)=1w(A(k)−∑i=1k(ki)w(i)C(k−i)).C^{(k)} = \frac{1}{w}\Big(A^{(k)} - \sum_{i=1}^{k} \left(\begin{matrix} k \\ i \end{matrix}\right) w^{(i)}\, C^{(k-i)}\Big).

The circle

Nurbs.kreis(mx, my, r) assembles a full circle from four quarter arcs: degree 2, nine control points on the circumscribed square (corners and edge midpoints), weights 1,22,1,22,…1, \tfrac{\sqrt 2}{2}, 1, \tfrac{\sqrt 2}{2}, \dots, knots (0,0,0,14,14,12,12,34,34,1,1,1)(0, 0, 0, \tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, \tfrac34, 1, 1, 1). Each quarter arc is a rational quadratic Bézier curve, which represents a conic section exactly.

Recomputed for the unit circle at u=1/8u = 1/8 (middle of the first quarter arc, basis values 14,12,14\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14):

C=14(1,0)+12⋅22(1,1)+14(0,1)14+12⋅22+14=(0.6036,0.6036)0.8536=(22,22).C = \frac{\tfrac14 (1, 0) + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt 2}{2} (1, 1) + \tfrac14 (0, 1)}{\tfrac14 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt 2}{2} + \tfrac14} = \frac{(0.6036,\ 0.6036)}{0.8536} = \Big(\frac{\sqrt 2}{2}, \frac{\sqrt 2}{2}\Big).

Surfaces of revolution

NurbsFlaeche is the tensor product of two NURBS directions,

S(u,v)=∑i,jNi,p(u)Nj,q(v)wijPij∑i,jNi,p(u)Nj,q(v)wij,S(u, v) = \frac{\sum_{i,j} N_{i,p}(u)\,N_{j,q}(v)\,w_{ij} P_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j} N_{i,p}(u)\,N_{j,q}(v)\,w_{ij}},

and rotation(meridian) builds from it the surface of revolution of a plane meridian curve (r(v),z(v))(r(v), z(v)) about the z axis (A8.1): the control net is the product of the nine circle points and the meridian points, Pij=(rjkx,i,rjky,i,zj)P_{ij} = (r_j\,k_{x,i},\ r_j\,k_{y,i},\ z_j), wij=wicirclewjmeridianw_{ij} = w^{\text{circle}}_i\, w^{\text{meridian}}_j. Because circle and meridian are exact, so is the surface: sphere (meridian: NURBS half circle), cylinder (straight line), cone (inclined line), torus (offset circle). Hub and casing of compressor and turbine annuli are exactly such surfaces.

Limits

  • PCHIP is only C1C^1 (curvature jumps at the nodes) and of lower order at extrema; for smooth, non-monotone data a cubic spline is more accurate.
  • Outside the nodes the classes behave differently: PCHIP continues the end polynomial, CubicSpline returns the constant end value, Akima1D extrapolates linearly. Extrapolation is not reliable in any case.
  • Global interpolation with B-splines is not local: one data point changes all control points. A least-squares approximation (fewer control points than data) does not exist.
  • NurbsFlaeche only evaluates points, no derivatives or normals; surfaces of revolution only about the z axis.
  • No curve length, no projection of a point onto the curve, no intersection.
