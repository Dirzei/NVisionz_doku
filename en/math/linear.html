Dense linear algebra – background

The computation behind LinAlg, MatrixSimple, JacobiEquilibrator and ConditionEstimator: LU decomposition with pivoting, Cholesky, least squares with Householder reflections, the Jacobi symmetric eigendecomposition, the scaling of the Newton solvers and condition estimation – with pseudocode following the source, worked examples, checks and limits.

What this is about

Almost every engineering computation ends up in a linear system Ax=bA x = b at some point: the Newton step of a thermodynamic cycle model, the regression line through measured points, the principal axes of a covariance matrix. Here AA is dense – almost all entries are non-zero – and small to medium sized, from two to a few hundred unknowns. Large sparse systems are handled by the sparse LU and the Krylov methods.

A computer does not solve Ax=bA x = b via the inverse; it factors AA into pieces that are easy to solve with: triangular matrices (LU, Cholesky), orthogonal matrices (QR) or diagonal matrices (eigen- and singular value decomposition). Which factorisation fits depends on the matrix:

Condition: what a result is worth

Before the methods, one quantity that bounds every computation below. If the right-hand side bb changes by δb\delta b, the solution changes by δx=A−1δb\delta x = A^{-1}\delta b, and in relative terms

‖δx‖‖x‖≤κ(A)‖δb‖‖b‖,κ(A)=‖A‖‖A−1‖=σmax⁡σmin⁡\frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|} \le \kappa(A)\,\frac{\|\delta b\|}{\|b\|}, \qquad \kappa(A) = \|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\| = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}}

The last expression holds for the 2-norm. σmax⁡\sigma_{\max} and σmin⁡\sigma_{\min} are the largest and smallest singular values. The condition number κ\kappa is the amplifier with which errors in the data carry over into the solution. In double precision (ε≈2.2⋅10−16\varepsilon \approx 2.2 \cdot 10^{-16}) the rounding errors of the input are of size ε\varepsilon; a backward stable method such as LU with pivoting then delivers a solution with a relative error of about κε\kappa\,\varepsilon. With κ=1012\kappa = 10^{12} four significant digits remain – the method is not to blame, the problem does not contain more.

This gives the ground rules of the package: no detour via the inverse where a factorisation suffices; no detour via ATAA^T A where AA itself can be factored (it squares κ\kappa); and balance the units before computing, because mixed units inflate κ\kappa artificially (section scaling).

LU decomposition with row interchanges

In step kk, Gaussian elimination subtracts multiples of row kk from all rows below it until only zeros remain below the diagonal. Keeping the multipliers gives a factorisation

PA=LU,lik=aik(k)akk(k),aij(k+1)=aij(k)−likakj(k)(i,j>k)P A = L U, \qquad l_{ik} = \frac{a_{ik}^{(k)}}{a_{kk}^{(k)}}, \qquad a_{ij}^{(k+1)} = a_{ij}^{(k)} - l_{ik}\, a_{kj}^{(k)} \quad (i, j > k)

LL is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal (Doolittle form), UU upper triangular, PP the row permutation. The diagonal element akk(k)a_{kk}^{(k)} is called the pivot. It is chosen by row interchange: the largest in magnitude among all |aik(k)||a_{ik}^{(k)}|, i≥ki \ge k. Then all multipliers satisfy |lik|≤1|l_{ik}| \le 1, and rounding errors are not amplified during elimination. Without this choice a tiny pivot can drive the multipliers up to 101310^{13} – exactly finding B15 in MatrixSimple (section MatrixSimple).

With the factorisation, Ax=bA x = b becomes two substitutions: first Ly=PbL y = P b from top to bottom (the diagonal of LL is 1, no division), then Ux=yU x = y from bottom to top. The determinant comes for free: det⁡A=±∏kukk\det A = \pm \prod_k u_{kk}, the sign being that of the permutation. The inverse is obtained by solving with the unit vectors as right-hand sides – nn solves with the same factorisation.

Pseudocode – LinAlg.luDecompose(lu, piv, grenze) and luSolve

// in-place factorisation; lu is a copy of A
piv[i] ← i for all i;  sign ← 1
for k ← 0 to n−1:
    p ← row i ≥ k with largest |lu[i][k]|              first one on ties
    if grenze = 0:  if |lu[p][k]| = 0: return NaN           exactly singular
    else:          if not |lu[p][k]| > grenze: return NaN
    if p ≠ k: swap rows p and k (also piv), sign ← −sign
    for i ← k+1 to n−1:
        f ← lu[i][k] / lu[k][k];   lu[i][k] ← f        multiplier of L
        for j ← k+1 to n−1:  lu[i][j] ← lu[i][j] − f · lu[k][j]
return sign

// solve
x[i] ← b[piv[i]]
for i ← 0 to n−1:   x[i] ← x[i] − Σ_{j<i} lu[i][j] · x[j]         forward, L
for i ← n−1 down to 0:
    x[i] ← (x[i] − Σ_{j>i} lu[i][j] · x[j]) / lu[i][i]            backward, U

Source: envisionz-framework-math/src/envisionz/math/linear/LinAlg.java, private methods luDecompose and luSolve. Rows are swapped as array references, not copied element by element.

Convention in the framework: solve(a, b), det and inverse declare a matrix singular only if a pivot is exactly zero – then the result is null (for det the value 0). solve(a, b, relTol) instead requires every pivot to exceed relTol⋅n⋅maxij⁡|aij|\text{relTol} \cdot n \cdot \max_{ij}|a_{ij}|; the optimisers call it with relTol = 1e-14. Both leave a and b unchanged.

Worked example

A=(2114−60−272)A = \left(\begin{matrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & -6 & 0 \\ -2 & 7 & 2 \end{matrix}\right), b=(5,−2,9)b = (5, -2, 9).

  2. Column 1: both candidates have magnitude 4, the first stays (no swap). Multiplier 4/4=14/4 = 1, remainder 2−1⋅1=12 - 1 \cdot 1 = 1.

L=(1000.510−0.511),U=(4−60041001),det⁡A=−1⋅4⋅4⋅1=−16L = \left(\begin{matrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 1 & 0 \\ -0.5 & 1 & 1 \end{matrix}\right), \quad U = \left(\begin{matrix} 4 & -6 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{matrix}\right), \quad \det A = -1 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 \cdot 1 = -16

Forward with Pb=(−2,5,9)Pb = (-2, 5, 9): y=(−2,5+1,9−1−6)=(−2,6,2)y = (-2,\ 5 + 1,\ 9 - 1 - 6) = (-2, 6, 2). Backward: x2=2x_2 = 2, x1=(6−2)/4=1x_1 = (6 - 2)/4 = 1, x0=(−2+6)/4=1x_0 = (-2 + 6)/4 = 1. Cholesky decomposition

If AA is symmetric and positive definite – stiffness matrices, covariance matrices, the Hessian approximation of an optimiser – there is a factorisation without pivoting at half the cost:

A=LLT,ljj=ajj−∑k<jljk2,lij=1ljj(aij−∑k<jlikljk)(i>j)A = L L^T, \qquad l_{jj} = \sqrt{a_{jj} - \sum_{k<j} l_{jk}^2}, \qquad l_{ij} = \frac{1}{l_{jj}}\Big(a_{ij} - \sum_{k<j} l_{ik}\, l_{jk}\Big) \quad (i > j)

Pivoting is not needed because every radicand is positive for a positive definite matrix and the entries of LL stay bounded by aii\sqrt{a_{ii}}. Conversely, a radicand ≤0\le 0 is the cheapest test whether a symmetric matrix is positive definite.

Pseudocode – LinAlg.cholesky(a) and choleskySolve(l, b)

for i ← 0 to n−1:
    for j ← 0 to i:
        s ← a[i][j] − Σ_{k<j} l[i][k] · l[j][k]          only the lower triangle is read
        if i = j:
            if s ≤ 0: return null                          not positive definite
            l[i][i] ← √s
        else:  l[i][j] ← s / l[j][j]

// solve: L y = b, then Lᵀ x = y
for i ← 0 to n−1:            y[i] ← (b[i] − Σ_{k<i} l[i][k] · y[k]) / l[i][i]
for i ← n−1 down to 0:      y[i] ← (y[i] − Σ_{k>i} l[k][i] · y[k]) / l[i][i]

Source: envisionz-framework-math/src/envisionz/math/linear/LinAlg.java, cholesky and choleskySolve. Used by the dual QP solver of the SQP.

Example. A=(422253236)A = \left(\begin{matrix} 4 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 6 \end{matrix}\right): l00=2l_{00} = 2, l10=1l_{10} = 1, l11=5−1=2l_{11} = \sqrt{5 - 1} = 2, l20=1l_{20} = 1, l21=(3−1)/2=1l_{21} = (3 - 1)/2 = 1, l22=6−1−1=2l_{22} = \sqrt{6 - 1 - 1} = 2. LinAlg.cholesky returns exactly these entries.

Least squares with Householder reflections

With more measured points than unknowns, Ax=bA x = b with AA of size m×nm \times n, m>nm > n, usually has no solution. We want the xx that minimises the sum of squared errors ‖Ax−b‖22\|A x - b\|_2^2. The obvious route via the normal equations ATAx=ATbA^T A x = A^T b squares the condition number. qrLstsq instead factors A=QRA = Q R with orthogonal QQ and upper triangular RR. Because orthogonal matrices do not change lengths,

‖Ax−b‖2=‖Rx−QTb‖2,\|A x - b\|_2 = \|R x - Q^T b\|_2,

and the minimum is at R1:nx=(QTb)1:nR_{1:n}\, x = (Q^T b)_{1:n} – a triangular system. QQ is never formed: each column is rotated onto a multiple of the first unit vector by a Householder reflection H=I−2uuT/(uTu)H = I - 2\,u u^T / (u^T u), and the same reflection is applied to bb immediately.

u=x−αe1,α=−sign⁡(x1)‖x‖2⇒Hx=αe1u = x - \alpha e_1, \qquad \alpha = -\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\,\|x\|_2 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad H x = \alpha e_1

The sign of α\alpha is chosen opposite to x1x_1 so that forming u1=x1−αu_1 = x_1 - \alpha causes no cancellation.

Pseudocode – LinAlg.qrLstsq(a, b)

r ← copy of a;  qtb ← copy of b
for k ← 0 to n−1:
    xnorm ← ‖r[k..m−1][k]‖₂
    if xnorm = 0: return null                            rank deficient
    α ← (r[k][k] > 0) ? −xnorm : +xnorm
    u ← r[k..m−1][k];  u[k] ← u[k] − α
    if uᵀu = 0: return null
    for j ← k to n−1:  r[·][j] ← r[·][j] − 2 (uᵀ r[·][j]) / (uᵀu) · u     reflect R
    qtb ← qtb − 2 (uᵀ qtb) / (uᵀu) · u                             and b
for i ← n−1 down to 0:  x[i] ← (qtb[i] − Σ_{j>i} r[i][j] · x[j]) / r[i][i]

Source: envisionz-framework-math/src/envisionz/math/linear/LinAlg.java, qrLstsq. No column pivoting: rank deficiency is only detected when a remaining column is exactly zero.

Example. Line y=c0+c1xy = c_0 + c_1 x through the points (0,1)(0, 1), (1,3)(1, 3), (2,4)(2, 4), (3,4)(3, 4): AA has the rows (1,xi)(1, x_i). qrLstsq returns c0=1.5c_0 = 1.5, c1=1c_1 = 1. Check via the normal equations: (46614)c=(12,23)\left(\begin{matrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{matrix}\right) c = (12, 23), determinant 20, c0=(168−138)/20=1.5c_0 = (168 - 138)/20 = 1.5, c1=(92−72)/20=1c_1 = (92 - 72)/20 = 1.

Symmetric eigendecomposition after Jacobi

A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors: A=VΛVTA = V \Lambda V^T. The Jacobi method (1846) reaches this form by successive plane rotations. A rotation in the plane (p,q)(p, q) with cosine cc and sine ss is chosen so that the element apqa_{pq} vanishes:

θ=aqq−app2apq,t=sign⁡(θ)|θ|+θ2+1,c=1t2+1,s=tc\theta = \frac{a_{qq} - a_{pp}}{2\,a_{pq}}, \qquad t = \frac{\operatorname{sign}(\theta)}{|\theta| + \sqrt{\theta^2 + 1}}, \qquad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{t^2 + 1}}, \qquad s = t\,c

t=tan⁡φt = \tan\varphi is the smaller root of t2+2θt−1=0t^2 + 2\theta t - 1 = 0; the rotation angle thus stays below 45° and the computation is stable. A rotation destroys zeros created earlier, but the sum of squares of the off-diagonal part off⁡(A)=∑p<qapq2\mathrm{off}(A) = \sum_{p<q} a_{pq}^2 decreases by apq2a_{pq}^2 with every rotation. One sweep rotates once over all pairs p<qp < q; after a few sweeps the method converges quadratically.

Pseudocode – LinAlg.eigSym(a)

A ← copy;  V ← I
tol ← 1e−16 · max(1, ‖A‖_F²)
for sweep ← 1 to 100:
    if Σ_{p<q} a_pq² ≤ tol: stop
    for all p < q:
        if |a_pq| < 1e−300: continue
        θ, t, c, s as above
        rotate columns p, q of A;  rotate rows p, q of A        A ← Rᵀ A R
        rotate columns p, q of V                                  accumulate eigenvectors
eigenvalues ← diagonal of A;  sort descending, carry the columns of V along

Source: envisionz-framework-math/src/envisionz/math/linear/LinAlg.java, eigSym and sortDesc. The comment in the code records that an earlier version using atan2 picked the wrong branch for app<aqqa_{pp} < a_{qq}.

Example. A=(2112)A = \left(\begin{matrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{matrix}\right): θ=0\theta = 0, t=1t = 1, c=s=1/2c = s = 1/\sqrt 2. A single rotation makes AA diagonal, the eigenvalues are 3 and 1 (eigSym returns 2.99999999999999962.9999999999999996 and 0.99999999999999980.9999999999999998), the eigenvectors (1,1)/2(1, 1)/\sqrt 2 and (1,−1)/2(1, -1)/\sqrt 2.

Convention in the framework: eigenvalues in descending order, eigenvectors as columns. The whole matrix is read; if it is not symmetric the result is wrong without any message. After 100 sweeps the method stops silently. For general matrices use EigenQr.

Singular value decomposition

Since the move (item ST3), LinAlg.svd(a) is a thin wrapper around GolubKahan.zerlege: A=Udiag⁡(s)VTA = U \operatorname{diag}(s) V^T, ss descending. It used to compute via the eigendecomposition of ATAA^T A and lost every singular value below about 10−8s010^{-8}\,s_0. The method is described on the page eigenvalues and SVD; the signs of individual pairs (uj,vj)(u_j, v_j) can differ from before, the factorisation is only unique up to this sign.

MatrixSimple: Gaussian elimination that passes rank defects on

MatrixSimple is the matrix of the Newton solvers in the eqnparser. It computes the same as the LU above – elimination with column pivoting, that is, choosing the largest entry of the current column – but has a different contract: a column without a usable pivot is not an error but is skipped and recorded. The solution component for that column stays 0; in Newton's method this means the affected variable stays at its start value, and the solver's diagnostics can name it via getLastPivotlessColumns(). This is the basis of the rank diagnosis of underdetermined models.

Pseudocode – MatrixSimple.solveLinear(rhs)

tol ← min(1e−12, max(1e−280, 2.2e−16 · max|A_ij|))      relative threshold, capped above
a ← copy of A;  b ← copy of rhs;  pivotless ← empty
row ← 0
for col ← 0 to ncols−1, while row < n:
    p ← row ≥ row with largest |a[p][col]|
    if |a[p][col]| < tol:  pivotless ← pivotless + col;  continue
    swap rows p and row in a and b
    for r ← row+1 to n−1:
        f ← a[r][col] / a[row][col]
        if f ≠ 0:  a[r][col..] ← a[r][col..] − f · a[row][col..];  b[r] ← b[r] − f · b[row]
    pivotcol[row] ← col;  row ← row + 1
x ← 0
for r ← row−1 down to 0:
    c ← pivotcol[r]
    x[c] ← (b[r] − Σ_{cc>c} a[r][cc] · x[cc]) / a[r][c]
return x                                              pivotless components stay 0

Source: envisionz-framework-math/src/envisionz/math/linear/MatrixSimple.java, solveLinear and pivotTol. inverse() and toReducedRowEchelonForm() compute Gauss-Jordan with the same pivot choice and the same threshold.

Convention in the framework: the pivot threshold is min⁡(10−12,max⁡(10−280,2.2⋅10−16max⁡|Aij|))\min(10^{-12}, \max(10^{-280},\ 2.2\cdot 10^{-16} \max|A_{ij}|)). It is thus scale-independent from below – a matrix whose entries are all around 10−1310^{-13} no longer counts as pivotless – and capped from above by the former absolute threshold 10−1210^{-12}, hence never stricter than the legacy behaviour. A sharper factor (tried: 10−1310^{-13}) declared a legitimate column with entries around 1.7⋅10−71.7 \cdot 10^{-7} at max⁡|A|=1.7⋅106\max|A| = 1.7 \cdot 10^6 pivotless in the combustor model (comment in the code).

Example. (123246101)x=(6,12,2)\left(\begin{matrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{matrix}\right) x = (6, 12, 2) – the second row is twice the first. Column 0: pivot 2 (row 1), after that the former first row is all zero. Column 1: pivot −2. Column 2: no candidate above the threshold → pivotless. solveLinear returns x=(2,2,0)x = (2, 2, 0) and getLastPivotlessColumns() = [2]; xx satisfies all three equations but is only one of infinitely many solutions.

Scaling the Jacobian

An engine model mixes pressures in pascal (10510^5), temperatures in kelvin (10310^3), mass flows (10110^1) and Mach numbers (10−110^{-1}). The Jacobian then has entries spanning many orders of magnitude, and its condition number is large although the physical problem is harmless. With diagonal matrices RR (per equation) and CC (per unknown) the system can be rewritten without changing the solution:

J˜=RJC,f˜=Rf,J˜Δx˜=−f˜,Δx=CΔx˜\tilde J = R J C, \qquad \tilde f = R f, \qquad \tilde J\,\Delta\tilde x = -\tilde f, \qquad \Delta x = C\,\Delta\tilde x

JacobiEquilibrator determines RR and CC from the current Jacobian, alternating between rows and columns, so that the largest entry of every row and every column becomes about 1:

ri=1maxj⁡|Jij|cj,cj=1maxi⁡ri|Jij|r_i = \frac{1}{\max_j |J_{ij}|\,c_j}, \qquad c_j = \frac{1}{\max_i r_i\,|J_{ij}|}

The class names the method after Sinkhorn and Knopp; their original iteration balances row and column sums, here the maximum norm is used. After the last column step every column has a maximum of exactly 1, every row at most 1.

Pseudocode – JacobiEquilibrator.compute(J, n)

c[j] ← 1 for all j
for iter ← 1 to 5:                                    MAX_ITER = 5, fixed
    for each row i:     m ← max_j |J[i][j] · c[j]|;   r[i] ← (m > 1e−14) ? 1/m : 1
    for each column j:  m ← max_i |r[i] · J[i][j]|;   c[j] ← (m > 1e−14) ? 1/m : 1
active ← true

applyToJacobian:  J[i][j] ← r[i] · J[i][j] · c[j]
applyToRhs:       f[i] ← r[i] · f[i]
unscaleStep:      dx[j] ← c[j] · dx̃[j]                  new array

Source: envisionz-framework-math/src/envisionz/math/linear/JacobiEquilibrator.java. Before compute the class is the identity; getRowScale and getColScale return copies.

Example. J=(10523⋅10−24⋅10−4)J = \left(\begin{matrix} 10^5 & 2 \\ 3\cdot 10^{-2} & 4\cdot 10^{-4} \end{matrix}\right). First row pass: r=(10−5,33.3)r = (10^{-5},\ 33.3); column pass: c=(1,75)c = (1,\ 75); further passes change nothing. Result J˜=(10.001511)\tilde J = \left(\begin{matrix} 1 & 0.0015 \\ 1 & 1 \end{matrix}\right). The condition number (2-norm, recomputed with GolubKahan) drops from 2.5⋅1082.5 \cdot 10^8 to 2.622.62. This JJ is case NM1-JE of the Linearprobe.

Condition estimation for the solver

The exact condition number requires a singular value decomposition – O(n3)O(n^3) in every Newton iteration. ConditionEstimator offers four cheaper quantities and a classification by which the eqnparser's solver switches to Levenberg-Marquardt.

How to read the four quantities follows from what they compute:

  • Frobenius is a true upper bound: κ2≤κF≤nκ2\kappa_2 \le \kappa_F \le n\,\kappa_2, because ‖M‖2≤‖M‖F≤n‖M‖2\|M\|_2 \le \|M\|_F \le \sqrt n\,\|M\|_2 holds for each of the two factors. For the identity matrix κF=n\kappa_F = n.
  • The heuristic estimateInfNorm is what the eqnparser's solver calls in every iteration. It is cheap but not an estimate of κ∞\kappa_\infty: it does not see linear dependence; the singular matrix (1111)\left(\begin{matrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{matrix}\right) gives 1, the JJ from the scaling example 5⋅1045 \cdot 10^4 instead of 2.5⋅1082.5 \cdot 10^8. Until 2026-09-28 its Javadoc promised a Higham estimator (finding WT3, corrected); the computation was kept on purpose because the solver's thresholds are tuned to it. Whether the solver switches to the real estimate is open (WT23).
  • The infinity-norm estimate estimateInfNormHager (new 2026-09-28) is Hager's estimator (1984) in Higham's version (1988, LAPACK xLACON): ‖J−1‖∞=‖J−T‖1\|J^{-1}\|_\infty = \|J^{-T}\|_1 is approached from below in at most five steps, each with one solve with JTJ^T and one with JJ through the same LU factorisation, plus Higham's extra vector of alternating signs. The result is a lower bound and usually exact: for the JJ of the scaling example it gives 2.5⋅1082.5 \cdot 10^8 (exactly κ∞\kappa_\infty), for the 6×66 \times 6 Hilbert matrix exactly 2.907⋅1072.907 \cdot 10^7; the singular matrix (1111)\left(\begin{matrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{matrix}\right) gives MAX_VALUE.
  • The diagonal ratio is an alarm for a diagonal going to zero (e.g. ∂F/∂Ma\partial F/\partial Ma near Ma=1Ma = 1), not a condition number; a single zero already gives MAX_VALUE (until 2026-09-28 only an all-zero diagonal, finding WT3, fixed).

Limits

  • Dense: memory n2n^2, cost n3n^3. Sensible up to a few hundred unknowns, beyond that the sparse LU or Krylov methods.
  • solve(a, b) without a threshold only detects exactly singular matrices. A nearly singular matrix yields a solution whose error can be κε\kappa\,\varepsilon – to rule that out, use solve(a, b, relTol) or check the residual.
  • No iterative refinement and no equilibration in LinAlg; scaling is the caller's job (JacobiEquilibrator).
  • qrLstsq has no column pivoting and detects rank loss only when a remaining column vanishes exactly. For ill-conditioned or rank-deficient least-squares problems Singulaerwertzerlegung.loese is the right route (minimum-norm solution).
  • eigSym assumes symmetry without checking it and stops silently after 100 sweeps.
  • MatrixSimple solves rank-deficient systems “around the gap” – the result is a solution, not the minimum-norm one; the caller must evaluate getLastPivotlessColumns().
  • ConditionEstimator.estimateInfNormHager is a lower estimate; in rare, constructed cases it lies below κ∞\kappa_\infty by a larger factor. For very large dense systems the LU factorisation costs O(n3)O(n^3).
