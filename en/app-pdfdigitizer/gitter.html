Table grid – rules, white space, merged cells

The computation behind GridDetector, Weissraumgitter and Tabellenerkennung: how a page image becomes rows and columns, why the longest ink run counts rather than the total, how bands split into text rows, how columns without rules come from free gutters, and how merged cells and sub-columns are recognised – with pseudocode after the source, a worked example, verification and limits.

What it is about

Before a single character is read, the cells have to be located. The result is a grid: ascending row boundaries y0<y1<…<yRy_0 < y_1 < \dots < y_R, ascending column boundaries x0<…<xCx_0 < \dots < x_C, and from them the cells [xc,xc+1)×[yr,yr+1)[x_c, x_{c+1}) \times [y_r, y_{r+1}). A cell spanning several rows is a merged cell; the cells below it are then absent.

Two routes lead to the grid. Printed tables usually have rules, and a projection of the ink onto straight lines finds them. Many tables have no rules or only a few; then the columns must come from the white space between the text blocks. Tabellenerkennung chooses:

Everything works on a boolean image of the ink: a pixel is dark when its luminance Y=(299R+587G+114B)/1000Y = (299R + 587G + 114B)/1000 lies below the ink threshold (default 128). Isolated specks have been removed and the page straightened beforehand; the page Preparing the page image describes that.

Rules: the longest run

For every image row ii (every image column for vertical rules) a run is formed: consecutive dark pixels, with gaps of at most maxGap pixels (default 6) bridged – scans have dropouts, and an interrupted rule is still a rule. A row carries a rule if there is a run with

ℓ≥fLandt≥0.75ℓ,\ell \ge f\,L \qquad\text{and}\qquad t \ge 0.75\,\ell,

where ℓ\ell is the length of the run including bridged gaps, tt the number of its dark pixels, LL the image width (or height) and ff the minimum fraction minLineFraction (default 0.5). The first condition separates rules from text: a text row has many dark pixels, but word spaces and letter gaps interrupt every run, and its ink total is irrelevant. A rule has few pixels but one very long run.

The second condition, the ink fraction, is needed because gap bridging also joins things that are not rules. In a monospaced face at low resolution, identical digit strokes of a column stand exactly above one another; they touch the horizontal rules of every row, and the gaps in between are shorter than maxGap. Without the ink fraction this became a “vertical rule”, and a table got 16 instead of 12 columns. A true rule is almost entirely ink; such a chain only about three quarters or less.

A rule is several pixels thick and produces hits in neighbouring rows. Hits at most mergeDistance (5) apart are merged into one rule at the middle of the group:

y=⌊ifirst+ilast2⌋.y = \left\lfloor \frac{i_{\text{first}} + i_{\text{last}}}{2} \right\rfloor.

The rules yield the cells; cells that are too narrow (under 8 pixels) or too flat (under 6) are dropped. On a region of the page the same computation runs on the crop and the result is shifted back; the minimum fraction ff then refers to the width of the region. That is why the region exists: when several table blocks sit above one another, the column rules of one block may run over only a third of the page height, and a minimum fraction that allowed this would take dense digit columns elsewhere for rules.

Pseudocode – GridDetector.findLines

for each image row i:

        if dark(i, j):
            run ← run + gap + 1;  ink ← ink + 1;  gap ← 0
            if run ≥ f·L and ink ≥ 0.75 · run:  best ← max(best, ink)
        else:

Splitting bands into rows

Many tables draw horizontal rules only at group boundaries – say every five rows. Such a band is split by the horizontal projection: for each image row yy the number n(y)n(y) of dark pixels between the outer column rules. A text row is a stretch with n(y)≥n(y) \ge minInk, ended by at least minGap image rows below it; the new boundary lies in the middle of the gap.

The vertical rules must be masked (two pixels around each column rule). A vertical rule carries ink in every image row; without masking there is never a gap and every band yields exactly one row – a fault that does not look wrong, because nothing simply changes.

Merged cells

When a column holds a value in a larger face or centred over three rows, it hides the row gaps of all columns in the projection over the full width. So each column is projected separately. A piece taller than

h>1.8⋅median⁡(hk)h > 1.8 \cdot \operatorname{median}(h_k)

(median of the piece heights of all columns) does not count for the rows; the rows come from the other pieces. The tall piece becomes one cell in the topmost of the rows it covers by more than 30 % of their height, and the cells below are absent. Without a tall piece the result is bit-identical to the projection over the full width.

Columns from white space

Without vertical rules there is nothing to project; the columns have to come from the gutters between the text blocks. First the text rows are determined (as above, over the ink box of the page or region), then for each image column xx the occupancy

b(x)=#{text rows with at least one dark pixel in column x}.b(x) = \#\{\,\text{text rows with at least one dark pixel in column } x\,\}.

Occupancy is counted per row, not as a pixel sum: a spanning heading carries many pixels but occupies only one row and so does not fill a gutter. A gutter is a strip with b(x)≤⌊0.12R⌋b(x) \le \lfloor 0.12\,R \rfloor (RR the number of text rows), at least

g=max⁡(8,round⁡(0.8⋅hz))g = \max\big(8,\ \operatorname{round}(0.8 \cdot h_z)\big)

pixels wide, hzh_z the median row height. The minimum width separates columns from word spaces: a word space is narrower than 0.8 row heights, even when it sits at the same place in every row – as it does in a column that always starts with the same word. The column boundary lies in the middle of the gutter; gutters at the edge of the ink box do not count.

Here too a tall piece distorts the computation: over the full width the projection merges several rows into one, hzh_z becomes a multiple, and the minimum gutter too wide. So hzh_z is also measured in eight narrow strips; if their median is lower by more than a factor of 1.8, it applies.

Sub-columns without a separating rule

A spreadsheet program can hide a vertical rule; then two columns stand between the same rules, say one left-aligned and one centred. After the rules route, each column is therefore searched for a gutter, with the same occupancy computation as for white space but referred to the rows of the grid. It is split only if

  • the gutter is at least gg wide and at most one row crosses it (with at least four rows – a header cell over both sub-columns),
  • on both sides at least ⌈0.25R⌉\lceil 0.25\,R \rceil rows carry ink,
  • and both sides fill the same rows: of the rows with ink on one side, at least 50 % must also have ink on the other.

The third condition came from a measurement. A footnote marker before the number – “(1) 86.8” – stands only in some rows; in a monospaced face the gap after it is wider than the minimum gutter, and the marker was cut off as a column of its own. True sub-columns are both occupied in almost every row; a marker only in some, its number in all.

A merged cell in a split column goes to the sub-column containing the centroid of its ink.

Worked example

An image row is 400 pixels wide, f=0.5f = 0.5, so a run must be at least 200 pixels long.

  • Rule with dropouts: 100 dark pixels, 3 light, 100 dark … After two pieces ℓ=203\ell = 203, t=200t = 200, fraction 0.985 – a rule.
  • Digit chain: strokes of 12 pixels, 5 apart. After nn strokes ℓ=17n−5\ell = 17n - 5; 200 is reached at n=13n = 13 (ℓ=216\ell = 216, t=156t = 156), fraction 156/216=0.722<0.75156/216 = 0.722 < 0.75. The fraction tends to 12/17=0.70612/17 = 0.706 as nn grows and never reaches 0.75 – not a rule. Without the fraction it would be one.
  • Merging: a rule 3 pixels thick hits image rows 99, 100, 101, a second one 150. Result: rules at ⌊(99+101)/2⌋=100\lfloor(99+101)/2\rfloor = 100 and 150.
  • White space: row height 20 gives a minimum gutter of 16 pixels; with 25 text rows, 3 may cross a gutter.
  • Merged cell: piece heights 18, 19, 20, 20, 21, 22, 60 – median 20, limit 36; the piece from 40 to 170 is tall. It covers rows 30–80, 80–130 and 130–180 by 40, 50 and 40 pixels, each more than 15 (30 % of 50) – a merged cell over three rows.

Verification

The tests draw their tables themselves, with rectangles instead of letters, so the result does not depend on the fonts of the machine: rules at known places, thick rules, bands of five rows, tables without rules with varying and aligned word spaces, a spanning heading, horizontal rules only, tall pieces over three rows, sub-columns with a header cell, footnote markers and digit chains. Every rule on this page has a counter-test: if it is broken in the source, a test fails.

Limits

  • Rules must lie almost horizontal and vertical. A skew of only 0.3 degrees made thin rules break into two image rows across the width; that is why the page is straightened first.
  • A small table on a page with running text does not reach the minimum rule fraction, and white space does not separate the columns of running text; the region helps there.
  • A tall piece with no rows beside it is a row itself – a single-row table in a large face stays correct, but a merged cell whose neighbouring columns are empty is not recognised as merged.
  • A column in which one sub-column is occupied in only a few rows is deliberately not split.
