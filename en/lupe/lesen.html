Country, year and face value

How the stamp reader turns a camera image into an announcement such as “Helvetia, 1964, value 20”: text lines in three orientations, the country via the per-character probabilities instead of the read text, year and face value by fixed rules, and a matrix code if the stamp carries one.

What this is about

A character recognizer rarely reads a postage stamp without errors: the writing is small, lies on a motif and is often rotated in the image. The reader therefore does not rely on the read text alone, but on what a stamp can contain: an inscription from a closed list, a year and a short number as the value.

Three orientations

Year and value usually stand horizontally on a stamp, but the stamp itself lies in the camera image at any angle. The reader therefore rotates the image by +90° and −90° and reads all three orientations separately. The orientation of the stamp is the one in which the year stands (unrotated first), otherwise that of the country; the value comes only from this orientation. A larger pseudo-number in another orientation therefore does not win.

The country via the probabilities

The read text of a country inscription is often useless, but the correct letter is usually in second or third place in the recognizer's output. The reader therefore searches every inscription a1…ama_1 \dots a_m of the list in the sequence of probability distributions p1…pnp_1 \dots p_n of a line, as a substring with free start and end (after Sellers). A character costs

c(i,j)=1−min⁡(1,pj(ai)+pj(a˜i)),c(i, j) = 1 - \min\bigl(1,\; p_j(a_i) + p_j(\tilde a_i)\bigr),

where a˜i\tilde a_i is the digit confusable with the letter (0 for O, 1 for I, 5 for S, 8 for B, 2 for Z, 6 for G). The recursion over the inscription (rows ii) and the read characters (columns jj) is

Di,j=min⁡(Di,j−1+0.9,Di−1,j+1,Di−1,j−1+c(i,j)),Di,0=i,D0,j=0.3j,D_{i,j} = \min\bigl(D_{i,j-1} + 0.9,\; D_{i-1,j} + 1,\; D_{i-1,j-1} + c(i,j)\bigr), \qquad D_{i,0} = i,\quad D_{0,j} = 0.3\,j,(1)

and the cost is K=minj⁡(Dm,j+0.3(n−j))K = \min_j \bigl(D_{m,j} + 0.3\,(n-j)\bigr). Characters at the edge of the line thus cost 0.3 instead of nothing: a half-sure short inscription should not find a cheap home in a long pseudo-line from the motif. An inscription counts as found if K/m≤0.6K/m \le 0.6, it has at least seven characters and the line is at least 0.7 of its length.

In addition the read text itself is checked: confusable digits are reinterpreted as letters, and an inscription may differ by at most a fifth of its length; inscriptions up to four characters only exactly. A longer inscription takes precedence over one contained in it.

Year and face value

A year is four digits between 1840 and the current year, also as part of a line; at least three of them must be real digits, so that no year arises from letters. The face value is a number of at most three digits standing alone in its line; of several, the largest writing wins. Only lines whose characters are all recognized with at least 0.5 count as a value.

Matrix code

Newer stamps carry a two-dimensional matrix code. The reader searches for it in the unprocessed image and adds its content to the reading. If it starts with the German identifier “DEA”, the country is Germany, even against a foreign country read from remnants of the motif. A foreign code does not change the country.

Limits

  • All thresholds (0.6 per character, seven characters, 0.5 certainty) are tuned on photos of a few real stamps.
  • The rule “largest writing wins” does not distinguish writing from motif: on the probe's generated stamp a pair of circles reads as a sure number and beats the correct value.
  • Year and value are not yet read from the matrix code.
